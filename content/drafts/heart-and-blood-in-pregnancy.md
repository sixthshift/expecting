A lot of pregnancy's work happens out of sight, in your heart and blood vessels. It explains why you can feel puffed or light-headed without having done much.

## Your heart works harder

To meet the growing demand for blood supply to your baby, your heart enlarges and pumps faster. The Royal Women's Hospital puts it this way: you're already doing an aerobic workout just by being pregnant. It also leaves a smaller gap between your resting heart rate and a safe maximum, so keep exercise to a moderate intensity.

## More blood, wider vessels

The volume of your blood rises as your baby grows. Your blood vessels also expand to increase blood flow to your baby, and hormones affect your circulation. These normal changes can lower your blood pressure: it commonly falls below normal in the first trimester, keeps falling in the second, then rises back towards your pre-pregnancy level by the birth. A slight drop in the first half of pregnancy is unlikely to cause problems for you or your baby.

Low blood pressure can make you feel tired, or faint. To help:

- drink plenty of water
- stand up slowly after sitting, or getting out of bed or the bath
- avoid standing for long; if you must, leg exercises and support stockings help blood flow
- eat at regular intervals
- lie on your side rather than your back
- if you start to feel faint, sit or lie down until it passes

## Blood pooling in the legs

Softer vein walls and, later on, the weight of your uterus can slow blood flowing back to your heart, so it pools in your legs. Calf raises and walking on the spot help, and support stockings can help if you tend to feel light-headed or have varicose veins.

## Lying on your back

After 16 weeks, avoid exercises lying on your back: the weight of the baby can press on the major blood vessels returning blood to your heart, and make you feel light-headed or sick. It's also recommended to sleep on your side in the second half of pregnancy.

## Iron and anaemia

You need more iron when you're pregnant, and iron deficiency is the main cause of anaemia in pregnancy, which happens in about 1 in 4 pregnancies. It can leave you weak, tired or dizzy, and cause headaches, shortness of breath or chest pain. Your haemoglobin is checked with a blood test at your first visit and usually again at about 28 weeks.

## When to get help

**Go straight to your nearest emergency department, or call 000** for an ambulance, if you're in early pregnancy and you faint or feel dizzy, or have bleeding or abdominal pain. It could be a sign of an ectopic pregnancy.

**If you feel dizzy or faint**, call your midwife, or Pregnancy, Birth and Baby on 1800 882 436 to speak to a maternal child health nurse (7 am to midnight AET, 7 days a week).

**Mention it at your next check** if you notice symptoms of low blood pressure.
