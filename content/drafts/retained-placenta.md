A retained placenta is when some of the placenta stays in your uterus after your baby is born. It happens in around 2 out of every 100 births. It's uncommon, but it can be a serious complication: if untreated, it can lead to severe infection or life-threatening blood loss.

## How the placenta usually comes

Birthing the placenta is the third stage of labour, and it can take anywhere from 5 to 60 minutes after your baby is born. With **active management**, your midwife or doctor gives you an injection of synthetic oxytocin to help your uterus contract, clamps and cuts the cord, gently pulls on the cord and may massage or press on the top of your belly. With **physiological (natural) management**, you wait for the placenta to come with the help of gravity and some contractions, which may take up to an hour. You can usually hold your baby through it.

## When it counts as retained

Your doctor or midwife will diagnose a retained placenta if it hasn't completely come out within **30 minutes** with active management (when you've had oxytocin), or **1 hour** with natural management. After it comes, they check the placenta to make sure it's complete; they need your permission for a vaginal examination.

It may be retained if contractions aren't strong enough to deliver it, the placenta has implanted too deeply into the wall of the uterus (placenta accreta), the cervix closes and traps it, or a full bladder is in the way. There is nothing you can do differently to prevent it.

## What's tried

If the placenta is slow, your midwife might suggest:

- emptying your bladder, by getting up to the toilet or with a catheter
- changing position
- breastfeeding or nipple stimulation
- gently pulling on the cord
- medicine

If that doesn't work, you may need surgery to remove it, usually under an epidural or general anaesthetic, so you won't feel any pain. While you wait, your midwife monitors your bleeding and general health. After a home birth you will likely need to go to hospital by ambulance. If you needed help to deliver your placenta, especially if you had a fever or needed surgery, your doctor will likely recommend antibiotics to prevent infection.

## Afterwards

You may stay longer in hospital, and recovery can feel harder when the birth didn't go to plan. Talk with your midwife about what happened and how you feel, or at your 6-week check.

## When to get help

A retained placenta can also show up days or weeks after the birth. Symptoms include fever, heavy bleeding, bad-smelling discharge and many large clots.

**Call triple zero (000)** and ask for an ambulance if the bleeding or pain is severe.

**Contact your midwife or doctor as soon as possible** if you have any heavy bleeding or pain in the days or weeks after the birth, or you pass many large clots, or your bleeding becomes heavier, bright red or foul-smelling.
