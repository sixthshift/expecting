Check with the cruise line before you book. Sea travel may be restricted towards the end of pregnancy, and MotherSafe says most cruise lines restrict travel beyond 28 weeks and have their own requirements for pregnant passengers.

## Why

- **Care on board.** If you fall ill, you'll usually be treated in the ship's medical facilities, which may not be up to Australian standards. If it's too serious, you may be transferred to a hospital at the next port or evacuated, and you may have to pay the bills, including the evacuation, on the spot.
- **Motion sickness and falls.** Sea travel can bring on motion sickness or nausea and vomiting, and with the higher risk of falls on a moving ship, MotherSafe says many people put off cruising until after the baby is born.
- **Infections.** COVID-19, flu and gastro are common on cruise ships, and respiratory and food-borne infections are a risk.

## What to do

- **Talk to your doctor or midwife** about the trip before you commit.
- **Insurance:** choose a policy that covers cruising and your onshore activities, and check it covers your pregnancy. Some ships won't let you board without cruise-specific insurance. Even a cruise that stays in Australian waters needs travel insurance: you may not be able to claim Medicare or private health on the ship.
- **Seasickness medicine:** check with your doctor, pharmacist or MotherSafe first.
- **Short trips** by boat or ferry are generally safe in pregnancy.
