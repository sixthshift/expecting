## What's usual

Feeling worried during a time of great change is normal. As many as 30 per cent of pregnant people feel some level of anxiety, and a smaller number have more severe symptoms. Many assume what they're feeling is just part of pregnancy and don't ask for help. You don't have to go through it alone.

It may be more than everyday worry if:

- worry or fear interrupts your thoughts and gets in the way of daily tasks
- worries keep coming back and are hard to stop or control
- you're on edge, irritable or restless most of the time
- you have tense muscles, a tight chest or a racing heart
- you have panic attacks
- it takes you a long time to fall asleep

Anxiety can start for the first time in pregnancy, or an existing condition such as generalised anxiety, OCD, panic disorder, social anxiety or PTSD can get worse.

It's treated well with talking therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy, relaxation training and mindfulness, and sometimes medicine, decided with your doctor. Things that may help day to day: deep breathing and relaxation, staying active, a calming bedtime routine, accepting practical help, and limiting time on unhelpful social media.

## Call 000 or go to emergency

If you're having thoughts of suicide and are in immediate danger, call triple zero (000). For help and support, call Lifeline on 13 11 14.

Beyond Blue says to seek help immediately or call triple zero (000) if you're having thoughts of suicide or harming yourself.

## See your doctor or midwife

See your GP or midwife if you, or the people around you, are worried about your anxiety, or if treatment isn't helping. Your midwife or doctor checks your mental health at your appointments; try to answer honestly. You can also call the PANDA helpline on 1300 726 306.
