The official pages we could read give no pregnancy-specific advice on lambing or farm work, so ask your doctor or midwife before you help with births. Meanwhile, Q fever infection in pregnancy can lead to miscarriage, stillbirth, premature birth and low birth weight, and being pregnant gives you a higher chance of the infection becoming long-lasting (healthdirect).

## Why

Q fever spreads from animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, dogs, cats and kangaroos, which often look healthy. It's most often caught by breathing in dust particles from dried urine, faeces, placenta or birth fluids. NSW Health says breathing in the bacteria while birthing, slaughtering or butchering infected animals (especially cattle, sheep or goats) carries a very high risk of infection. You can also catch it by handling animals, wool, hides, straw or clothing that has touched them. Toxoplasmosis can also be found in sheep and kangaroos.

The Q fever vaccine is not routinely recommended in pregnancy: it isn't thought harmful, but its safety in pregnancy hasn't been established. It's recommended for people in high-risk jobs, such as farmers, shearers, abattoir and vet workers.

## What to do

- **Wash your hands** after touching animals.
- **If you work with animals**, wear a fitted P2/N95 mask and gloves, cover wounds, and take off work clothes before going home.
- **Unpasteurised milk:** healthdirect says drinking it may also increase your chance of developing Q fever.
- **Follow your employer's** health and safety advice.
- **See your doctor as soon as possible** if you get symptoms of Q fever (fever, severe headaches, extreme tiredness) after contact with animals, and tell them about the contact.
