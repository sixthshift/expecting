Labour is described in three stages. They're common to every labour, but everyone experiences them differently, and if you've had a baby before, this birth may go differently. Some hospitals call the recovery afterwards a fourth stage.

## First stage: the cervix opens

The cervix (the neck of the uterus) thins, softens and opens until it's fully open at about 10 cm. It's the longest stage. RANZCOG says it usually takes about 12 hours in a first labour and 8 hours in later ones.

- **Latent phase (early labour).** The cervix shortens, thins and begins to open. It can last hours or even days. Contractions may be regular or irregular, or you might not notice them. You can usually stay home.
- **Active phase.** The cervix opens from about 4 cm to 10 cm. Contractions are stronger and more regular, around 3 to 4 minutes apart. You may be advised to go to hospital or the birth centre during this phase.
- **Transition.** At about 8 to 10 cm. You may feel pressure in your bottom or the urge to push, shaky legs, nausea, find it hard to talk, or feel you "can't do it anymore".

When you arrive, the midwife feels your tummy for the baby's position, asks about your contractions, listens to the baby's heartbeat, checks your blood pressure and pulse and tests your urine. With your consent, they may do a vaginal examination to see how far the cervix has opened.

## Second stage: pushing and the birth

From full dilation to the birth. Contractions are usually longer, with longer breaks between them. You may feel strong pressure in your bottom, an urge to push, and stretching or burning in your vagina. Your midwife may suggest a vaginal examination to check you're fully open, but there are other signs they can look for too; you can talk with them about how you'd prefer to be assessed.

The Royal Women's Hospital says pushing can last up to two hours, usually less if you've had a baby before; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says the second stage can last up to 3 hours, and an epidural can make it longer.

## Third stage: the placenta

After the birth, the placenta and membranes come away. You can have an injection of oxytocin to help (active management, up to about 30 minutes) or let it happen without one (physiological, up to about an hour). The cord is clamped and cut; tell your midwife ahead of time if you'd like it clamped later or your partner to cut it.

## After

Your midwives check you and the baby, feel that your uterus is contracting, check for tears or heavy bleeding and stitch any tears, and help with feeding if you choose.
