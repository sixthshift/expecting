## What's usual

Your metabolism speeds up in pregnancy to support your baby, which raises your body temperature and makes you more sensitive to hot weather. 

It's important to avoid overheating, particularly in the first trimester, when becoming overheated can affect your baby's growth and development: when you're hot, more blood goes to your skin to cool you, and less may reach the placenta.

To stay cool:

- stay out of direct sun, and inside or in the shade in the middle of the day; find air conditioning if you can
- drink regular sips of water, and wear comfortable, cool clothing
- spray water on your face and body, put a wet washer around your neck, or your hands and feet in cool water
- have a cool bath or shower

**Heat exhaustion** comes from losing a lot of fluid: heavy sweating, feeling very thirsty, a headache, muscle aches or cramps, dizziness, a faster pulse, pale clammy skin. Dark wee is a sign of dehydration. Lie down somewhere cool and shady, loosen tight clothes, put your hands and feet in cool water or wet your skin, sip water, and avoid soft drinks, alcohol and coffee. Left untreated it can become heatstroke.

## Call 000 or go to emergency

Call triple zero (000) immediately and ask for an ambulance if you have symptoms of heatstroke, such as hot, dry skin with no sweating or a rise in body temperature to above 40 °C. Heatstroke requires immediate medical treatment, as it can be life-threatening.

Get emergency medical care if you have symptoms of heatstroke, such as:

- hot, dry skin with no sweating
- a sudden rise in temperature to over 40 °C
- severe dehydration
- rapid breathing
- vomiting
- confusion or slurred speech
- loss of consciousness or seizures

## See your doctor or midwife

See a doctor or go to an urgent care clinic if you get dizziness, nausea, a fast pulse or shortness of breath. See a doctor if heat exhaustion doesn't improve after you've moved somewhere cooler and rested.
