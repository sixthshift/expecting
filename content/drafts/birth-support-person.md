A birth support partner stays with the person giving birth through labour and birth, giving emotional and physical support. It's a big responsibility and a privilege, and it matters: the right support can shorten labour, reduce how much pain relief is needed, lower the chance of an assisted birth or caesarean, and boost confidence and the experience of labour. You're there to help, not simply to watch.

## Before the day

- **Learn what to expect.** Go to antenatal classes, read about labour and birth, and tour the birth facility and ask questions.
- **Talk through the birth plan,** and what to do if things don't go to plan. Ask how and when your partner wants you to speak to the staff on their behalf.
- **Check how many support people are allowed** in the room with the doctor, midwife, hospital or birth centre.
- **Be honest if you can't do it.** If you don't think you can physically or mentally give the support needed, say so beforehand. They might choose someone else, ask you for part of the labour, or want more than one support person. A backup person is a good idea anyway, in case you're unwell or away.

## In early labour

Stay with them so they're not alone. Go for a walk together, encourage them to eat and drink, time the contractions, and help with massage and resting positions.

## As labour goes on

- encourage and comfort them
- support them in labour and birth positions
- guide their breathing and other ways of coping
- massage, hold hands, wipe their face, put a cool compress on their forehead
- offer snacks, drinks and ice; help them into the bath or shower

## Speaking for them

You may need to be their advocate. Tell the maternity team what your partner needs and wants, talk through with them what the team advises, keep them up to date as labour progresses, help them make decisions, and update family if they'd like.

Every birth is different. Be prepared to be flexible: preferences can change, even during labour, and sometimes medical interventions are needed.

## Look after yourself

It can be exhausting. Rest when you can, bring your own snacks and drinks, and swimwear if you'll be in a birthing pool or shower.

## A doula as well

Some people also have a doula: a non-medical companion who gives emotional and practical support. A doula doesn't replace the birth support partner; they work with the partner to support the person giving birth. Doulas don't give medical advice, aren't regulated, and charge a private fee.
