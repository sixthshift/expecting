If you've lived through abuse or other trauma, pregnancy can reawaken it. That response is normal, and there's support made for it.

## Why it comes back

Many people feel a loss of control over their body in pregnancy, which can echo the loss of control during abuse. Routine antenatal checks and intrusive procedures can trigger memories. The Royal Women's Hospital says survivors of sexual assault can find it very difficult to be touched, check-ups involving the vagina can be very stressful, and fear of the birth can bring muscle tension that makes labour pain harder to manage. Birth itself can trigger flashbacks, particularly of sexual or domestic abuse, and some people dissociate during labour. Breastfeeding, changing nappies and bathing the baby can stir memories too.

A history of trauma can mean more anxiety and flashbacks as pregnancy goes on. Signs of complex trauma include flashbacks and nightmares, memory lapses, finding it hard to manage emotions, always being on edge, sleep problems and avoiding certain people or places.

## Trauma-informed care

You can expect trauma-informed care: care that recognises and responds to trauma, keeps you physically and emotionally safe, and is given with you, not to you. It uses neutral, supportive language, and you should never feel judged or labelled. It supports you to make informed choices through shared decision-making and informed consent.

## What you can do

- **Talk to your midwife, obstetrician or GP** about your fears of the birth; a specialist mental health clinician can help too.
- **Prepare a list** of your concerns and questions before appointments, and take a trusted person with you if you like.
- **Talk to a lactation consultant or your midwife** before or during breastfeeding if it's hard.
- **Find a trauma-informed perinatal clinician.** COPE has a directory, and the Gidget Foundation offers free individual psychological therapy for expectant, new and potential parents (up to 10 sessions per calendar year, subject to Medicare eligibility).
- **Set boundaries** about who cares for your baby, with a trusted support person's help.

If you're not happy with your care, start by talking to your doctor or midwife; you can also make a complaint.

## When to get help

If you notice symptoms of complex trauma, such as those above, it's a good time to reach out to a perinatal mental health clinician; start with someone you trust. Early identification and intervention are so important, according to the Gidget Foundation.
