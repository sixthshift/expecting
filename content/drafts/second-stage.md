The second stage starts when your cervix is fully open at 10 cm and ends when your baby is born. It has two parts: a passive part, when you're fully open but don't feel an urge to push (usually with an epidural), and an active part, when you feel the urge.

## The signs

- strong, longer contractions with longer breaks between them, to rest and build energy for each push
- more pressure in your bottom and an urge to push, often pushing without meaning to
- stretching or burning in your vagina, and the baby's head moving down
- a "purple line" between the buttocks in some births, grunting, or the head or membranes becoming visible

Your midwife or doctor may ask to do a vaginal examination to check you're fully open, but some signs don't need one. You can talk with them about how you'd prefer to be assessed.

## Pushing

Push with your contractions when you feel ready and your midwife says you're fully dilated; your body usually tells you when. Pushing is more effective upright: squatting, kneeling, sitting, standing, or in a pool or bath. RANZCOG recommends an upright position in the second stage, as it lowers the chance of an assisted birth; with an epidural, lying on your left side may be recommended.

**With an epidural,** you may not feel an urge to push, or it may be weaker, so your midwife will guide you on when and how long to push. An epidural limits some movement and can make this stage longer.

## How long

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says birth is generally expected within 3 hours of full dilation for a first birth, or 2 hours if you've had a baby before. With an epidural, that usually includes about an hour of passive descent, then 1 to 2 hours of pushing for a first birth.

If birth isn't close after 2 hours in a first birth, or up to an hour if you've had a baby before, your team will call a doctor to review. RANZCOG's guideline recommends escalation after 2 hours of pushing in a first birth or 1 hour in a later one, says that if you and the baby are well it's reasonable to support another hour of pushing, and says decisions to speed up the birth shouldn't be based on time limits alone.

## The birth

As the baby is born, your midwife or doctor helps ease the head out. With your consent, they may press gently on the head so it comes slowly, lowering the chance of tearing, and offer a warm compress on your perineum. If they think it's necessary, they may ask your permission for an episiotomy (a cut). If pushing is hard, the baby's position is tricky or they're distressed, forceps or a vacuum cup may be used.

Once the baby is born and well, they're given to you to hold, skin to skin.
