In a vaginal examination (VE), the midwife or doctor feels inside your vagina to check your cervix: how far it has opened (dilation) and how thin it has become (effacement). It can also show how far the baby's head has come down into your pelvis.

## When it's offered

- **Before labour is clear:** your midwife can tell whether you're having Braxton Hicks or are in labour by doing a VE.
- **When you arrive:** with your consent, a VE to measure how open your cervix is.
- **In the first stage:** Safer Care Victoria's guideline has staff offer one about every 4 hours to check progress. RANZCOG's guideline says they shouldn't plan to offer VEs more often than every four hours in the first stage; more may be recommended if there are concerns about you or the baby, or if you ask.
- **At other times:** before an opioid (narcotic) pain reliever or an epidural, or to confirm you're fully dilated if in doubt.

Safer Care Victoria notes that VEs more often than every four hours could increase the risk of infection for you and the baby.

## Your consent

Consent, written or spoken, must be obtained before any examination. Safer Care Victoria reminds staff that a VE can be painful and confronting, and to keep your dignity and privacy.

In the second stage, there are signs your midwife and doctor can assess to know you may be in the second stage, and some of them don't involve a VE.

## What the numbers mean

- **Up to 4 cm** is the latent (early) phase, in RANZCOG's guideline.
- **From about 4 or 5 cm** is active labour; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby uses 4 cm and RANZCOG's guideline 5 cm.
- **10 cm** is fully dilated: the start of the second stage.

Progress isn't judged on the cervix alone: your midwife or doctor also looks at your contractions, how far the baby has moved down, your wellbeing and your birth preferences. RANZCOG's guideline counts labour as slow when the cervix opens less than 2 cm in four hours from 5 cm.
