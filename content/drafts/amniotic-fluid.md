Amniotic fluid is the watery liquid that surrounds and protects your baby in the uterus. It's held in the amniotic sac, and it's what people mean by "the waters".

## Where it comes from

In the early weeks, your body makes it. As your baby grows, they make most of it themselves, from their urine and fluid from their lungs.

## What it does

It helps:

- your baby move around in the uterus
- your baby's lungs, digestive system and muscles develop
- keep the temperature around your baby normal
- cushion and protect your baby from pressure or injury

## How much is normal

Healthy amounts range widely, from 500 to 2000 mL. The amount rises through pregnancy until 34 weeks, and it varies between people and between pregnancies.

## How it's measured

It's checked on ultrasound, often at your routine scans. Your healthcare professional looks for pockets of fluid around your baby, and the amount is usually given in centimetres, as the amniotic fluid index (AFI). If you're unsure what your result means, ask your midwife or doctor.

## Too much or too little

Sometimes there's more or less fluid than expected. This can be due to a pregnancy complication, and may sometimes be a sign of a problem. The words you may hear:

- **Oligohydramnios:** low, or not enough, fluid.
- **Anhydramnios:** no fluid seen on the ultrasound.
- **Polyhydramnios:** more fluid than expected.

What it means for you and your baby depends on the cause, if known, how severe it is and how far along you are. Your doctor or midwife will explain your result, its likely causes and how it will be managed.

When the sac breaks, that's your waters breaking: see Waters breaking.
