How labour feels, and what helps, is very individual. Some people prefer medicines, others would like to avoid or delay them, and you can choose one method or a few. There's no right answer here, only what suits you.

## The options

**Without medicines**

- **Moving around** and changing positions (active birth). Being able to move freely can help you cope with contractions, and staying upright lets gravity help the baby down.
- **Relaxation and breathing**, and music if it helps.
- **Heat and water.** Warm or cold packs, a shower or a bath. Some hospitals also offer birth in the water.
- **Touch and massage**, to ease muscle tension and distract you.
- **Continuous support** from a partner, family member or midwife can help you feel safe, calm and better able to manage contractions.
- **TENS**, a small battery device with pads on your back that sends small electrical pulses. You can't use it with a pacemaker or spinal rods.
- **Sterile water injections** for lower back pain: four small injections just under the skin that cause a short bee-sting feeling and may help lower back pain, without any side effects for you or your baby (RANZCOG).

**With medicines**

- **Gas** (nitrous oxide, "laughing gas"), breathed through a mask or mouthpiece during contractions. It takes the edge off and wears off in about a minute. You may feel nauseous or light-headed or have a dry mouth; there are no long-term effects for you or your baby.
- **Opioids** such as morphine, by injection. They reduce the pain but don't take it away completely. They can cause drowsiness, nausea and trouble emptying your bladder, and may make you feel less alert. Some aren't recommended late in labour because they may affect the baby's breathing and alertness after birth.
- **An epidural**, given by an anaesthetist: very effective relief, with trade-offs of its own (see its own entry). It isn't available at a home birth.

## Things to weigh

- **What each one asks of you.** After an epidural you need to stay still, so moving around and water are off the table. You can't use water for 4 hours after an opioid. Opioids may not be recommended once labour is advanced.
- **What your birth place offers.** Not every birth unit has a birthing pool, and not every place offers every method. Ask what's available where you're booked, and which can be combined.
- **Changing your mind** during labour is completely normal. Tell your team and your support people. Your team will do its best, but it isn't always possible to switch from one type to another.
- **Your support person** can offer reassurance and massage, help you change positions, and suggest relaxation and breathing, so talk your preferences through with them before labour too.

## When to decide

Before labour begins. Discuss your preferences with your midwife or doctor and your support people, and put them in your birth plan: your preferred method, alternatives in case things change, and anything you'd rather avoid. Learn about all the options, even ones that aren't in your plan, because plans can change. An antenatal class can help.
