A breech baby is lying bottom first or feet first instead of head first. Early in pregnancy this is very common, and it's normal until about 35 to 36 weeks: most babies turn head down before the last month. Near the due date only about 3 in 100 babies are still breech. Your midwife or doctor will feel your belly at around 36 weeks, and may send you for an ultrasound to confirm.

## The three kinds

- **Frank or extended:** bottom down, legs straight up in front of the body, feet near the face.
- **Complete or flexed:** sitting with legs crossed and feet near the bottom.
- **Footling:** one or both feet hanging below the bottom, so a foot comes first.

## Why it happens

Breech is more likely with a low-lying placenta, lax or weak uterine muscles (often after several babies), too much or too little amniotic fluid, an unusually shaped uterus or large fibroids, twins, a short umbilical cord, or a previous breech. Most commonly, though, no cause is found. If your baby is still breech near the due date, the chance of them turning without help is low.

## Turning the baby (ECV)

In an external cephalic version, an obstetrician presses gently on your belly to help your baby turn a somersault to head first. The sources differ on timing: RANZCOG says that at 36 weeks your obstetrician or midwife should discuss it; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says it's usually recommended after 37 weeks, or after 36 weeks for a first baby.

- It succeeds about half the time, more often if you've given birth before. A second attempt on another day may be possible.
- You may be given medicine to relax your uterus; it won't harm the baby. It can be uncomfortable but shouldn't be painful. Your baby's heart rate is monitored before and after.
- About 1 in 200 need an emergency caesarean straight after, because of bleeding from the placenta or changes in the baby's heartbeat, so it's done where that's possible.
- If your blood type is rhesus (RhD) negative, you'll get an anti-D injection afterwards.
- It isn't suitable for everyone: for example, if you need a caesarean anyway, have bled in the past 7 days, your waters have broken, or you're carrying twins.

Exercises, acupuncture, moxibustion and chiropractic have no evidence that they work.

## If your baby stays breech

You have two options: a planned caesarean, or a planned vaginal breech birth. Most breech babies at term are now born by caesarean. A planned caesarean is safer for the baby in the short term; in the longer term the baby's health is similar either way. A vaginal breech birth is possible in some cases, in a hospital with experienced staff, and around 4 in 10 people planning one end up needing an emergency caesarean. Not every hospital offers it. The choice has its own entry.

## When to get help

**If your waters break and you've been told your baby isn't head first, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.**

After an ECV, contact your doctor or midwife immediately if you have tummy pain, contractions, bleeding or fluid leaking from your vagina, or your baby is moving less than usual.
