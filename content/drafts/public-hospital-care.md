Public hospital maternity care is the most widely used model of maternity care in Australia. Public maternity hospitals generally give very good care in pregnancy, at the birth and afterwards, and fees and expenses are low if you have a Medicare card.

## How it works

- **Check-ups** mostly happen at the hospital's outpatient antenatal clinic, at the hospital itself or at an outreach clinic in the community. The clinic does your pregnancy care, scans and blood tests.
- **Who you see:** midwives and doctors, sometimes with physiotherapists, social workers or dietitians. You might not see the same midwife or doctor each time, and the ones you see in pregnancy often won't be there at the birth, except in team midwifery or caseload care (the Royal Women's).
- **Midwife-led or obstetrician-led:** care ranges from midwife-led care for low-risk pregnancies to obstetrician-led care if you have medical or pregnancy risk factors, such as pre-eclampsia.
- **The birth** usually happens in the hospital, with midwives and doctors working together. Afterwards, midwives and doctors look after you in hospital, and some follow-up may be at home or in the community. If you go home early, midwives will usually visit.

## Options inside the public system

Hospitals often offer more than one way of being cared for. Ask yours what it has:

- **Shared care:** your check-ups are shared between the hospital and a local GP or midwife affiliated with it.
- **Team midwifery:** a team of midwives cares for you, so you're more likely to get to know them, though you won't always see the same one.
- **Midwifery group practice (caseload):** one known midwife, with a small backup team, through pregnancy, birth and after. It's publicly funded, and the birth may be in hospital, a birth centre or sometimes at home.

## Things to weigh

Good points: a choice of models, care with very little intervention but medical back-up if it's needed, services such as dietitians and physiotherapists, and, in major public hospitals, facilities for high-risk pregnancies and very sick babies.

Downsides: parking can be hard, waiting times can be longer than at a private clinic, you're more likely to share a room after the birth, and you can't always choose a male or female practitioner.

## Getting started

Book into the maternity unit at your chosen hospital as soon as your pregnancy is confirmed; your first appointment will most likely be between 10 and 16 weeks. If your pregnancy is healthy, the Royal Women's says you're usually advised to book in at your closest maternity hospital.
