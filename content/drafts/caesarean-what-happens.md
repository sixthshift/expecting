A caesarean is an operation to birth the baby through a cut in your abdomen and uterus, usually low, near the bikini line. Around 4 in every 10 babies born in Australia in 2023 were born this way. Whether it's planned or an emergency, what happens in theatre is very similar.

## Getting ready

There are a lot of people in theatre, each with a job: the obstetrician and an assistant, the anaesthetist, nurses, a paediatrician and a midwife. Before the operation:

- your pubic hair may be shaved and your belly cleaned with antiseptic
- you're fitted with compression stockings to lower the risk of blood clots
- a cannula (drip) goes into your hand or arm for fluids and medicines
- a catheter goes into your bladder to keep it empty; with the anaesthetic you shouldn't feel it go in

## The anaesthetic

Usually an epidural or a spinal block, which numbs the lower body so you're awake but feel no pain. A spinal works very quickly but can't be topped up; an epidural can. Sometimes, especially in an emergency, a general anaesthetic is used and you're asleep: if a spinal or epidural isn't working, there isn't time, it isn't safe, or you ask for one.

## The operation

A screen goes across your chest so you can't see. Some doctors offer a "maternal assisted" caesarean, where the screen is lowered for part of it. You'll feel tugging and pulling but no pain or sharpness, and hear the waters being suctioned. The baby is usually born in the first 10 minutes. The operation takes about 30 to 40 minutes, and you're usually in theatre for about an hour (the Royal Women's Hospital says an hour or more in an emergency). The doctors talk you through it.

## Your support person

Usually one support person, such as your partner, can be with you, in scrubs, a theatre hat and closed-in shoes. Ask whether they can take photos or video. If you need a general anaesthetic, they won't be in the room.

## The baby

The midwife or paediatrician dries the baby and checks them. If the baby is well, you can have skin-to-skin on the operating table, with warm blankets and a bonnet, since theatre is cold. If the baby needs help breathing, the paediatrician cares for them in the room. Most hospitals have a midwife stay with you and the baby in theatre and recovery.

## Afterwards

You're looked after in the recovery room until you're ready for the ward. Some hospitals keep the baby with you there for skin-to-skin, and you can often breastfeed within an hour. You'll be offered pain relief. After a general anaesthetic you may not be able to hold or feed the baby straight away, but you can once you're more alert. Most people stay 3 to 5 days.
