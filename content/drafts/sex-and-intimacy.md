Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says your feelings about sex may change as your body goes through the stages of pregnancy, and that it's OK if you or your partner don't want to have sex: there are many ways to experience love and intimacy together.

## How desire changes

- **First trimester.** Sex may be the furthest thing from your mind. Tiredness and nausea are common and can get in the way, and breast tenderness may reduce your desire for contact involving your breasts or nipples.
- **Second trimester.** You might notice an increase in your sex drive, with less tiredness or nausea, hormone changes and extra blood flow to the genitals. It's also OK if desire stays low or drops.
- **The last months.** You might notice a drop, from tiredness, stress about giving birth or finding a comfortable position. It's perfectly OK not to feel like it.

Sex should only happen when you both agree to it.

## If you're worried about the baby

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says having sex does not harm your baby, unless your maternity care provider has told you otherwise. Partners are told that sex might feel different but will not harm the baby. If either of you has doubts, speak with your maternity care provider.

## Staying close

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says it's normal for couples to feel disconnected or anxious during pregnancy, and suggests talking about how you feel and about other ways to feel wanted and secure, such as kissing, cuddling and massage. The Gidget Foundation, writing about life after a baby, suggests nurturing emotional intimacy by making time to share thoughts and feelings, being open about the changes, and keeping a sense of humour.

## After the birth

It changes again. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says how long you wait is completely up to you; you may feel like it soon after, or it may take weeks or even months. Desire is influenced by physical recovery, emotional recovery, hormonal changes and breastfeeding. The Gidget Foundation says intimacy often changes after a baby, and suggests talking about it openly.
