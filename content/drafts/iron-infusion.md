Yes, if your doctor recommends it, but not in the first trimester: healthdirect says IV iron should be avoided then.

## Why

Tablets are usually tried first. If you don't get better with them, your doctor may offer iron straight into a vein. healthdirect lists the reasons: you can't take tablets, they aren't working, or you need your iron up quickly, for example late in pregnancy or to avoid a blood transfusion.

Side effects and severe reactions are very rare. The most common are headache, dizziness, flushed skin, nausea, muscle or joint pain, a metallic taste, and changes to blood pressure or pulse. Some start a day or two later and settle by themselves. Fewer than 1 in 100 people have a severe allergic reaction. Very rarely, the iron leaks under the skin around the drip and changes its colour.

## What to do

- **Tell your doctor you're pregnant,** and about any past reaction to iron.
- **On the day,** eat normally, take your usual medicines and drink plenty of water. A nurse watches you during the infusion and for 30 minutes after.
- **Tell the nurse or doctor straight away** if you have swelling of the lips, mouth or throat, trouble breathing or chest pain, or any pain, swelling or change in skin colour around the drip.
- **Ask about side effects** to expect. You'll need a blood test 2 to 4 weeks later to check your iron.
