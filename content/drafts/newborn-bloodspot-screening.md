Every baby is offered newborn bloodspot screening, often called the heel prick test. It checks for some rare genetic conditions that are more easily treated if found early. It's done within the first 72 hours after birth. It's free, and it's not compulsory, but it is recommended.

## Your consent

A sample can't be taken without your consent: verbal or written, depending on your state or territory. Your health team will give you information first, so you can ask questions before you agree. You can refuse the test on your baby's behalf, but this could risk their health. Most babies screened don't have any of the conditions; for the few who do, early treatment can improve their health and prevent severe disability or even death.

## What happens

A midwife or nurse pricks your baby's heel with a very thin needle and puts a few drops of blood on a special filter paper, which dries and goes to the screening laboratory. The heel prick isn't comfortable for your baby. The best thing you can do is comfort and hold them during it; breastfeeding has also been shown to soothe them.

## What it looks for

The conditions include congenital hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, phenylketonuria (PKU), MCAD deficiency, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) and severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).

## The results

If the results are normal, you won't be contacted. You'll only hear if the doctors are concerned. Some babies need a second blood test, usually because the first didn't give a clear result. If a test shows your baby might have one of the conditions, you'll be told as soon as possible, and your baby may need more tests or an appointment with a specialist.

Samples are stored securely for at least 2 years. After that, you can ask for your baby's to be returned to you or destroyed.
