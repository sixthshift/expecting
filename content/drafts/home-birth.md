Most births in Australia happen in hospital, but a planned home birth can be a safe option too, if your pregnancy is low risk and you're cared for by registered midwives with a plan to get you to hospital if there's a problem.

## Two ways to do it

- **A public hospital home-birth program.** Not many public hospitals have one; a few attach it to their midwifery group practice, and you go to the hospital for your check-ups. You need a referral from your doctor. With a Medicare card, care in a publicly funded home-birth program is covered by Medicare.
- **A private midwife.** You don't usually need a referral to book directly. It's expensive: Medicare may cover some pregnancy and postnatal care from a midwife with a Medicare provider number, but not the birth itself.

Either way, two registered health professionals with the right skills must be there for the labour and birth, usually two midwives, or a midwife and a doctor.

## Who it's for

A planned home birth isn't considered safe for every birth. It may be safe for you if all of these are true:

- your pregnancy is low risk, and stays low risk to 36 weeks or more
- you're cared for by an appropriately trained registered midwife
- there's a smooth plan to get you to hospital safely and quickly if problems arise.

Your doctor or midwife will recommend a hospital birth if, for example, you're expecting twins or more, your baby is breech, you've had a caesarean before, you're over 40, or you have certain medical conditions.

## Things to weigh

Good points: familiar surroundings, more privacy, the support people you choose, a midwife you know, and, for low-risk pregnancies, a higher chance of a vaginal birth without assistance and lower chances of a caesarean, instruments or serious perineal damage. Evidence shows little or no difference in safe outcomes for babies between planned home and planned hospital birth.

Downsides: no epidural or nitrous oxide gas at home, and you may need to transfer to hospital. If a serious complication happens in labour, you'll need to be urgently transferred, which can be harder than if you were already in hospital; the most common emergencies are heavy bleeding and a baby who doesn't breathe at birth. First-time parents are more likely to need a transfer.

## What to do

- Ask your local hospital or a private midwifery practice whether they offer home birth.
- **Book into your local hospital as a backup**, and make sure your midwife has a plan to get you there fast. Ask how long a transfer would take and which hospital you'd go to.
- Be prepared to go to hospital if your midwife thinks it's necessary.
- Make sure your baby gets vitamin K, the hepatitis B vaccine, the heel prick test and a hearing screen, or a referral to a service that does them.

## Home birth is not freebirth

Freebirth is giving birth without a midwife or other registered health professional. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says it can increase the chance of health problems for you and your baby, and complications may go undetected, even after a straightforward pregnancy. In a joint statement of 3 November 2025, RANZCOG (the obstetricians' college) and the Australian College of Midwives say: "The absence of appropriate clinical support during freebirth has led to preventable harm and loss of life." They define freebirth as "the intentional practice of giving birth without a registered healthcare professional, such as a midwife or doctor, present", and say it "is different from a homebirth, which is a planned birth at home with a registered healthcare provider". They have asked health ministers to restrict managing labour and birth to registered practitioners.

In an emergency, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.
