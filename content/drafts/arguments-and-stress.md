A baby will change your relationship in some way; that's guaranteed. Gidget says no-one is totally prepared for parenting, and that seems to be the main reason for early dissatisfaction. Work and family are hard to juggle, there's less time together, and intimacy often drops. Some couples get through with a few disagreements; for others it's a real struggle. It's normal to argue now and then, in pregnancy as at any time, and there are reasons to feel closer too.

## Where it often starts

PBB suggests talking early about the things that tend to cause friction: how you each feel about the pregnancy and what's coming, your parenting styles, money worries, your sexual needs, how you'll make time for yourselves and each other, and how you'll share the household tasks now and after the birth. Unwanted advice from family can be another: you can explain gently that some decisions are only yours to make.

## Talking it through

- **Pick the time.** Choose a time when you're both calm and won't be interrupted. It's OK to stop if either of you is angry, emotional or overwhelmed, and come back to it.
- **Say it from your side.** Use "I feel hurt" rather than "you're hurtful", and avoid "you always" and "you never". Explain rather than blame.
- **Listen, don't fix.** Gidget's advice is to listen and understand what's going on for your partner and try not to fix things quickly. Repeat back what you heard, ask questions.
- **Stick to the one problem,** not old mistakes.
- **Agree a next step together,** compromise, and give it time to work.
- **Respect different communication styles,** and give each other space after a disagreement if that's what's needed.

## Conflict isn't the verdict

Gidget says conflict is an essential part of any relationship. It can be a time to reconnect, build trust and sort things out. What matters most is the repair afterwards. Notice and say what you appreciate about each other: people in successful relationships make many more positive statements to each other than negative ones. Partners can help by taking on the domestic front without being asked and taking the baby to give the other a break; the person who's pregnant can acknowledge that partners struggle too.

## When it's more than arguing

The extra stress can flare up old problems, and Gidget notes it can increase the risk of domestic violence. Family violence can begin or get worse in pregnancy. For support with domestic and family violence, call 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732 (24/7). If your life is in danger, call 000.

## Where to get help

- Relationships Australia: 1300 364 277
- MensLine Australia: 1300 78 99 78, with counselling and tips on managing conflict
- Family Relationships Advice Line: 1800 050 321, for counselling and mediation
