Ask first. Most radiation exposure during medical testing is unlikely to harm your baby, but tell your doctor and the radiology practice that you are, or might be, pregnant before any test.

## Why

Any harm depends on the dose (smaller is safer), the stage of pregnancy (the further along, the better) and where on your body it's aimed: tests of your abdomen or pelvis carry a higher risk than others. For scale, Australians get about 2 mSv of background radiation a year; a chest x-ray is up to 0.1 mSv and a CT scan up to 8 mSv. It's best to avoid exposing your tummy to radiation, and CT scans are best avoided in pregnancy because of a small chance the radiation could harm the baby. MRI uses no radiation and is generally safe in pregnancy, but not in early pregnancy.

## What to do

- **Say you're pregnant** (or might be) to your doctor and the radiology staff before any test. Your doctor will assess whether you need it.
- **Ask about alternatives.** Ultrasound or MRI don't use ionising radiation and may be useful alternatives in some situations.
- **Had one before you knew?** Talk to your doctor as soon as you can. They can work out the dose to the baby. A single low dose, such as an x-ray, is often not likely to be harmful.
- **Nuclear medicine scan?** Check with your health team before breastfeeding later on.
