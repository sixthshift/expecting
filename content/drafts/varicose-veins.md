## What's usual

Up to 4 in 10 pregnant people get varicose veins, and they often start in the first trimester. You make more blood than usual, your uterus presses on the veins returning blood from your legs, and pregnancy hormones soften vein walls. They mostly show in the legs but can also affect the vulva or rectum (in the rectum they're called haemorrhoids). Your legs may ache, feel heavy, throb or burn, usually worse by the end of the day.

They usually get better without treatment by the time your baby is 3 to 4 months old, so laser and surgery aren't usually recommended during pregnancy. To ease the aches:

- wear compression stockings, professionally fitted
- do ankle exercises, and shift your weight from foot to foot when standing
- put your legs up when you can, and lie on your left side
- apply cold packs, avoid standing for long, and avoid tight clothes or high heels

Varicose veins raise your risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Talk to your doctor or midwife if you're worried, or if you may already be at risk of DVT for other reasons.
