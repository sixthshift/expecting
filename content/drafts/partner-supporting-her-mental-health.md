When the person you love isn't themselves, it's hard to know what to say, and easy to worry about getting it wrong. You don't need the perfect words, and you don't need the answers: you're a support person, not a health professional. What you do matters, though. For people with anxiety or depression around a birth, strong practical support from a partner can significantly improve their recovery, and many who are at risk of depression will turn to their partner for support.

## Starting the conversation

Choose a quiet time, put your phone away, and be calm, kind and genuine. Talking can be easier side by side, on a walk or over a cup of tea. You could say:

- "I've noticed you don't seem like yourself. How have you been feeling lately?"
- "There's no right or wrong way to feel during this time. How has it really been for you?"
- "It's okay if you don't have the words. I'm here for you."

If they're not ready to talk, go at their pace, let them know you're there, and check in again in a few days.

## What helps

- **Listen.** Spend time listening without feeling you need to offer solutions. Unasked-for advice can feel like criticism, even when it's well meant.
- **Tell them they're doing well.** Let them know how well they're doing, as a parent and as a partner, and support the things they do to look after themselves.
- **Keep them company.** Sit with them for a while. Put what they need ahead of your own jobs.
- **Practical help, agreed first.** Cooking, cleaning, a meal or a drink: offer, but talk it through first. Help that isn't agreed on doesn't always go down well. It can be very hard for someone who is anxious or depressed to know what they need.
- **Talk about getting help.** Talk together about whether to reach out to others. If you're worried, encourage them to see a health professional: their GP, midwife or a mental health clinician.
- **Look after yourself too.** Take time out. The PANDA helpline (1300 726 306) is there for support people as well.

## When to get help

If they're having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, get help immediately or call 000.

After the birth, if they suddenly seem confused or not themselves, are seeing or hearing things that aren't there, have strange beliefs, can't sleep for days, or you're worried about the baby's safety, this could be postpartum psychosis. It's rare, but they need to see a doctor urgently. If anyone is having thoughts of self-harm or you're worried about the baby's safety, go to the nearest emergency department or call 000 and ask for an ambulance.
