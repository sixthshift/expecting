Rainbow families come to parenthood through donor conception, fertility treatment, surrogacy, co-parenting, fostering, adoption or earlier relationships. Many describe it as deeply meaningful, and sometimes more intentional because of the planning involved. Children of same-sex parents do as well as any other children; what matters is what parents do.

## The care you deserve

Gidget says you deserve care that recognises your family structure without assumptions, uses inclusive language, and uses each person's pronouns and the parenting terms you prefer. A medical system largely geared to heterosexual couples and cisgender women can make that harder, and safe, appropriate care for trans, gender-diverse or intersex parents isn't always available. Where you can, choose health professionals, antenatal classes and parent groups that are inclusive. Some families feel most comfortable with services for their community, or with practitioners from it.

## Explaining yourselves

Comments, assumptions and having to explain your family can be tiring, especially at a time of change. It's okay to set boundaries. You don't need to explain your family to everyone.

## Between the two of you

Keep talking about feeding, leave, night wakes, money, appointments, and how each parent wants their role recognised at home and beyond. Bonding doesn't always happen instantly, especially for a parent who isn't genetically related to the baby; it grows through everyday care.

## Paperwork

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says same-sex parents are assessed for entitlements in the same way as everyone else. Who is recorded on the birth certificate is a matter for your state or territory's Births, Deaths and Marriages registry. In Victoria, for example, the birth mother's female partner can be added when the birth is registered, and if you consented to donor treatment together you're generally both recorded. If you used a known donor, Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says to consider getting legal advice, and that you can have a contract drawn up so everyone understands the agreement.

## Support

Research suggests LGBTQIA+ parents may have higher rates of perinatal depression and anxiety, from discrimination, a lack of support, the cost and disappointments of conceiving, and isolation. If worry, low mood or overwhelm lasts more than two weeks or makes daily life harder, talk to your GP, midwife or child health nurse.

- Rainbow Families runs peer support, community groups and inclusive antenatal education.
- QLife offers free, anonymous LGBTIQ+ peer support and referral: 1800 184 527, 3pm to midnight, every day.
- The Gidget Foundation offers perinatal psychological therapy.
