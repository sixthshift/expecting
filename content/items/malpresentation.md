## When to get help

**If your waters break and you've been told your baby isn't head-first, get medical help immediately.** There's a risk of cord prolapse, which is a medical emergency. If you feel the cord in your vagina, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.

## What it is

The ideal way for a baby to lie for birth is head-first, chin tucked, with the crown of the head against the cervix. Any other way is called a malpresentation, and it raises the chance of complications for you and the baby, a more complex vaginal birth, or a caesarean. More than 9 in 10 babies in Australia are head-first at birth.

## The positions

- **Breech:** bottom or feet first, the most common.
- **Face:** head-first but with the neck stretched back, so the face is against the cervix.
- **Transverse lie:** lying sideways, so the back, shoulders, arms or legs may enter the birth canal first.
- **Oblique lie:** lying diagonally, with no particular part against the cervix.
- **Unstable lie:** the baby keeps changing position after 36 weeks.
- **Cord presentation:** the cord lies against the cervix, between the baby and the birth canal.

It's more likely with a low-lying placenta, too much or too little fluid, an unusually shaped uterus or large fibroids, many previous pregnancies, twins or more, a first baby, a past breech, or preterm labour. Often no cause is found. Your midwife or doctor finds it by feeling your belly, confirmed by ultrasound if they're unsure.

## What can be done

If the baby is still breech at 36 weeks they're less likely to turn by themselves, and your obstetrician may recommend an ECV to gently turn the baby head-first. Different postures, acupuncture or moxibustion have limited evidence; there's some evidence moxibustion before 37 weeks may reduce the chance of a breech birth. Talk to your maternity carer before trying them.

If your baby is lying sideways or keeps changing position, your midwife or doctor may recommend staying in hospital from 37 weeks, so help is at hand when your waters break or labour starts.

## The birth

Most babies with a malpresentation close to birth are born by caesarean. A breech baby may be born vaginally in a hospital that can offer specialised care; whether that's safe for you depends on the baby's size and position, any past caesarean, and your hospital's expertise.

If you had a malpresentation in one pregnancy, you have a higher chance of it happening again.
