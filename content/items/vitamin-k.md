Vitamin K helps blood to clot. Babies don't get enough of it during pregnancy or from breast milk, and without it they're at risk of vitamin K deficiency bleeding (VKDB). VKDB is very rare, affecting fewer than 1 in 100,000 babies, but it can be very serious: it can cause bleeding in the baby's brain, which may lead to brain damage or death. It mainly affects breastfed babies. By about 6 months, babies have their own supply.

Health authorities in Australia recommend that all babies are given vitamin K, including babies born early or sick.

## The options

- **One injection** soon after birth, within a few hours. This is what Australian guidelines recommend, because it's the simplest and most reliable way for the baby to get the vitamin K they need.
- **Oral drops**, given into the mouth with a small syringe. The baby needs 3 doses, each at the right time: at birth, at 3 to 5 days, and at 4 weeks if fully breastfed. The body doesn't absorb vitamin K as well this way. Some babies can't have drops, including babies born early or unwell, or if you took certain medicines in pregnancy (for epilepsy, blood clots or tuberculosis).
- **No vitamin K.** Parents can choose. If you decide not to, watch closely for signs of bleeding.

## Things to weigh

- Vitamin K has been given to babies in Australia for more than 30 years with no known problems. A suggested link with childhood cancer was investigated thoroughly in Australia and none was found.
- After an assisted birth (forceps or vacuum), your doctor or midwife may tell you the injection is very important, because there's a higher chance of a bleed.
- It's free: Medicare covers the injection and the drops.
- With drops, remember to arrange the third dose at 4 weeks. If the baby vomits within an hour of a dose, they may need another.

Whatever you choose, see your doctor, midwife or child health nurse if the baby has unexplained bleeding or bruising, or yellow skin or eyes after 3 weeks old.

## When to decide

During the pregnancy. Your doctor or midwife will talk it through with you, offer it and give you written information, and it's given only with your consent. Ask them any questions you have.
