In midwifery group practice, also called caseload midwifery or continuity of carer, you have one midwife you get to know well. They see you through pregnancy, are on call for your birth, and may keep caring for you until 6 weeks after. One or two other midwives back them up when they're not available.

## How it works

Care is publicly funded. Your check-ups and the visits after the birth may be in hospital, in the community or at home. The birth usually happens in a hospital or birth centre, or sometimes at home, depending on your circumstances and what's offered locally. Some practices offer a homebirth option.

If a medical concern or risk factor comes up, your midwife works closely with the doctors and other specialists so you get the care you need.

## Why people want it

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby lists the benefits: a more positive experience of labour and birth, fewer interventions in labour, being more likely to have a vaginal birth and to breastfeed successfully. There is strong evidence that it lowers the chance of a premature baby, by nearly a quarter with midwifery continuity of care, and by about half for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families.

## Getting a place

At the Royal Women's, caseload is popular and not available in all locations, and you check with the midwife at your booking appointment whether it's available to you. If you have particular health concerns, you may not be eligible for some options.

So ask early: ask your GP or midwife at your first antenatal visit which models your area offers, and ask again at booking.

## If you can't get a place

**Team midwifery** is the same idea with less continuity: a small team of midwives cares for you, so you're less likely to see the same one each visit, but you'll usually get to know them all and one of them will be at your birth. A **private midwife** is another way to have one midwife throughout. Or ask your local maternity service what else it offers.
