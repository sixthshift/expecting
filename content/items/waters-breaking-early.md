## When to get help

**If your waters break and you've been told the baby isn't head-first, call triple zero (000)** and ask for an ambulance: there's a higher risk of cord prolapse.

**If you feel something in your vagina after your waters break, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.** The cord may have slipped down (cord prolapse), which is life-threatening for the baby. While you wait, get onto your hands and knees, then lower your head and chest so your bottom is in the air, higher than your head. This takes pressure off the cord. Stay like this until help arrives.

**If you think your waters have broken early, call your doctor or midwife immediately.** You'll need to be examined straight away. Put a clean pad in your underwear to collect the fluid. Fluid that smells bad, or is green, brown or bright red, could mean you and the baby need urgent attention.

## What it is

In about 4 in every 100 pregnancies the waters break early: before 37 weeks and before labour starts. It's called preterm prelabour rupture of membranes, or PPROM. You might feel a big gush, a slow leak, or just damp underwear. Amniotic fluid usually smells sweet and is clear or pink. It can be hard to tell apart from discharge or urine, and your midwife or doctor can test it.

## How they check

They'll ask about the fluid, look at your pad, and may look in your vagina with a speculum. If there's doubt, a test on the fluid gives a result in 5 to 10 minutes.

## What happens in hospital

The sac can't be closed once it's broken, so you'll be asked to stay in hospital for monitoring and treatment:

- antibiotics to prevent infection
- under 36 weeks, 2 steroid injections to help protect the baby's lungs
- under 30 weeks, with the birth expected in the next 24 hours, a magnesium sulphate drip to help protect the baby's brain
- blood and urine tests and vaginal and anal swabs for infection, plus a CTG and ultrasound to check the baby

The risks include infection in the uterus, cord prolapse, premature labour, the placenta coming away early, and the baby's lungs not developing fully.

## Going home

If you and the baby are well and you're not in labour, you might go home after a few days; some go home after 72 hours with no signs of labour or infection. At home, check your temperature every 4 hours, watch the baby's movements and the fluid, change pads every 4 hours, shower rather than bath or swim, and avoid tampons, sex and putting any medicine in your vagina.

Tell your doctor or midwife immediately if the baby's movements change, you have a fever or feel unwell, you have tummy pain, the fluid changes colour, smell or amount, or you start bleeding.

## When the baby comes

There's a 1 in 2 chance of labour starting within 24 to 48 hours, and most people go into labour within 7 days. You may be able to wait for labour to start, but if you or the baby is unwell, an earlier birth may be safest. Your doctor or midwife will weigh the risks of an early birth against staying pregnant; it's generally best to wait until 37 weeks, depending on your circumstances.
