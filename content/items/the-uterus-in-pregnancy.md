Your uterus (womb) sits in the middle of your pelvis, behind your bladder and in front of your rectum, held in place by ligaments. It's pear-shaped, with four parts: the fundus (the top), the corpus (the main body), the isthmus (the section between the body and the cervix) and the cervix (the lower part, which connects to your vagina). Its wall has three layers: an inner layer (the endometrium), a middle layer of muscle and a thin outer layer.

## How it grows

During pregnancy your uterus greatly increases in size. At 20 weeks the fundus is usually roughly level with your belly button, and by 36 weeks the uterus reaches all the way up to your ribcage. If your baby then engages in your pelvis, your bump may move down slightly.

## Fundal height

Fundal height is the distance from your pubic bone to the top of your uterus. Your doctor, obstetrician or midwife may measure it at each antenatal visit from 24 weeks. The measurement in centimetres should be about the same as the number of weeks you're pregnant: at 20 weeks, about 20 cm.

It can differ if you're carrying more than one baby, or living with overweight or obesity or certain health conditions. A smaller than expected measurement could be a sign that your baby is growing slowly or that there's too little amniotic fluid; a larger one could mean the baby is larger than average. In either case your doctor will monitor you and your baby carefully, and may recommend ultrasound scans.

## Practice contractions

Your uterus rehearses with Braxton Hicks contractions: a weak or uncomfortable tightening or ache around the bump lasting 20 to 90 seconds. They don't dilate your cervix, you may not feel them until the second trimester, and they can become more intense or frequent towards the end. They disappear when you change position or activity, unlike labour contractions, which get stronger, more regular and more frequent.

## In labour and after

In labour the uterine muscles contract in waves from the top down to the cervix, opening it and moving your baby down the birth canal. After the birth the uterus keeps contracting, which can feel like period pain ("afterbirth pains") and helps it shrink; you may feel them when you breastfeed.
