## What's usual

If you've had chickenpox before, or the vaccine, you're unlikely to catch it again. The Immunisation Handbook recommends checking immunity in pregnancy, so you may already know from a blood test.

Chickenpox is more serious in pregnancy. The most common complication is a lung infection, varicella pneumonia, which affects up to 2 in 10 pregnant people with chickenpox, about a week after the rash appears. It's more likely if you smoke, have a lung condition, have more than 100 blisters or have a weakened immune system. The baby can be affected too: chickenpox in the first 28 weeks carries a small chance of fetal varicella syndrome (scarring, eye problems, small limbs, or brain and nerve problems), and chickenpox near the birth can make the newborn seriously ill.

The Handbook counts it as a significant exposure if you:

- live with someone who has chickenpox or shingles
- were face to face with them for at least 5 minutes
- were in the same room as them for at least an hour

Someone with chickenpox is infectious from 2 days before their rash appears until every blister has crusted over. Shingles is less catching than chickenpox, but still counts. Symptoms, if you catch it, usually start about 2 weeks after (10 to 21 days).

## See your doctor or midwife

- **If you've been near someone with chickenpox or shingles,** contact your doctor or midwife straight away, even if you have no symptoms. If you're not immune, you may be offered an injection of antibodies (zoster immunoglobulin). The Handbook says it should be given within 96 hours (4 days) of the exposure, though it may still help up to 10 days after. A blood test can check your immunity first, if there's time.
- **If you think you have chickenpox** (an itchy red rash that turns into blisters, often with a fever, headache or sore throat), call your doctor straight away. Don't go to the clinic: you could infect other people in the waiting room. Call, and they'll tell you the safest way to see them. Antiviral medicine can make it less severe. Your doctor will suggest a detailed scan about 5 weeks after the infection.
- **See your doctor urgently** if you have chickenpox and develop a high fever, neck pain, difficulty breathing, sore red areas around the blisters, or changes to your vision or eye pain.

**For the partner:** if you've never had chickenpox or the vaccine, ask your doctor about having it. A pregnant person who isn't immune is not a reason to delay vaccinating others in the household.
