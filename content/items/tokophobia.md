Fear before giving birth is a normal emotion. It's common to be anxious about pain, interventions in labour or harm to your baby. You might also fear the unknown, not getting to hospital in time, or pooing during the birth. There are ways to prepare that help.

## Common fears, and what helps

**The unknown.** Every labour is different. Talk to your doctor and midwife about your choices; they can help you make a **flexible** birth plan: whether you'd like an intervention or not, who you want with you, staying active, and positions for labour and birth.

**Pain.** There are medical and non-medical kinds of pain relief, and your doctor or midwife can go through them with you before labour. Being informed and involved in decisions can help you feel more in control and cope better. **You can change your mind about pain relief during labour**, and your team will support what's right for you at the time.

**Interventions.** Sometimes a baby needs help to be born, by caesarean, an episiotomy or an assisted birth. Talking to your team, antenatal classes and reading can help you understand the options beforehand.

**Not making it in time.** The first stage of labour may take longer than you expect, so there's usually plenty of time, and very few births in Australia happen outside a hospital or birth centre. Keep the maternity unit's number handy, have your bag packed, and know how you'll get there.

**Pooing during the birth.** It's common, many people don't notice, and your midwife has seen it all before.

**A previous hard experience.** A traumatic birth, miscarriage, stillbirth, termination, fertility problems or neonatal death can all come back in a new pregnancy. Talk about it with your doctor and midwife before the birth: they can guide you to support and offer trauma-informed care.

## Other things that help

Exercise, learning about birth, antenatal classes, visiting the maternity ward, meditation, yoga or relaxation, and talking with someone you trust: a counsellor, your midwife or doctor, your partner, other parents or family.

## When it's more than fear

For a very small number of people, fear of childbirth becomes overwhelming. This is **tokophobia**. Primary tokophobia usually starts in childhood, without any experience of birth; secondary tokophobia follows a traumatic pregnancy or birth. Some people also have anxiety, depression or PTSD during or after pregnancy. You're not alone, and help is available.

## When to get help

If you feel fearful and overwhelmed, or your fears are affecting your life, contact your doctor, PANDA on 1300 726 306 or Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636. If you need to talk to someone about your mental health, you can also call Lifeline on 13 11 14.
