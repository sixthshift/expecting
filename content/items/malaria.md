Avoid travelling to areas with malaria if you can. Malaria is spread by infected mosquitoes in parts of Africa, Asia, Central and South America, the Pacific and Eastern Europe.

## Why

Pregnant people are among those most at risk of severe malaria. In pregnancy it can lead to miscarriage, stillbirth, premature birth or a small baby, and to anaemia or even death for the pregnant parent. Pregnant people are also more likely to be bitten by mosquitoes (MotherSafe).

Where malaria resists treatment, you may need tablets to prevent it (RANZCOG), and even then they don't always work (healthdirect). Which tablets suit you, your pregnancy and your itinerary is your doctor's call.

## What to do

- **If the trip can't wait,** see your doctor or a travel clinic 4 to 6 weeks before you go, and tell them you're pregnant. They'll advise on medicines for your itinerary.
- **Avoid bites:** use a repellent with DEET, IR3535 or icaridin (picaridin); MotherSafe says medium-strength DEET is preferred to high strength (avoid 80%), and RANZCOG says to use a spray rather than a roller, sprayed over your clothes, so less goes on your skin. Wear long sleeves and long trousers in the evening, sleep under an insecticide-treated net or in a screened room, and avoid outdoor activities after sunset (MotherSafe says to minimise outdoor activities from dusk to dawn).
- **Fever during the trip or after you're home:** see a doctor urgently. If you think you might have malaria, see your doctor or go straight to the emergency department. Symptoms usually start 10 to 15 days after infection, but some types come back months or years later.
