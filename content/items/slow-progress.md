Slow progress (sometimes called prolonged labour) is when labour takes longer than expected. It can happen in the active first stage or the second stage. The evidence on what's "normal" and what's "slow" isn't strong, and experts define it differently.

## What counts as slow

**First stage.** Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says the active phase may take up to 8 to 18 hours with a first baby and 5 to 12 hours after that, and counts it as slow when the cervix opens less than 2 cm in 4 hours. It also counts a latent (early) phase as slow if it lasts longer than 20 hours in a first labour, or 14 hours after that. RANZCOG's guideline defines slow labour as less than 2 cm in four hours from 5 cm.

**Second stage.** Pregnancy, Birth and Baby counts it as slow if the baby isn't born after 2 hours of full dilation or 1 hour of pushing with the urge in a first birth, or after 90 minutes of full dilation or 30 minutes of pushing if you've had a baby before. RANZCOG's guideline says decisions to speed up the birth shouldn't be based on time limits alone, and that another hour of pushing can be reasonable if you and the baby are well.

## Why it happens

You may be more likely to have a slow labour if you're dehydrated or have a full bladder, it's your first birth, you've had an epidural or induction, you've had a slow labour before, you can't move about much, you're older, shorter than average or living with obesity, or your uterus is shaped differently. The baby may be very large, lying in an unusual position, or have a health concern.

## What can help

Things you can try: changing position, moving around and staying active, relaxation, and pain relief if you need it.

Depending on the stage of labour and your situation, your team may offer:

- **breaking your waters**
- **an oxytocin drip** to make contractions stronger. RANZCOG calls breaking the waters or a drip with an artificial hormone augmentation: it may be needed when labour has started but contractions aren't regular or strong enough to open the cervix
- **turning the baby's head by hand** into a better position

RANZCOG's guideline says breaking the waters, or breaking them early with an oxytocin drip, shouldn't be routine when labour is progressing without complications. For slow labour, breaking the waters with an oxytocin drip may be considered, after it's explained that its effect on how long labour lasts and how the baby is born is uncertain.

If the baby needs help to be born, forceps or a vacuum, an episiotomy, or a caesarean may be recommended.

## Your choice

Your midwife and doctor will explain the risks of slow labour to you and the baby, and the risks and benefits of each option. It's your choice whether to have any intervention.

## Why it matters

Slow labour can lead to complications, including severe tears, heavy bleeding after the birth, infection, and a lack of oxygen for the baby. That's why you and the baby are watched closely.
