A due date is an estimate. A birth anywhere from 37 to 42 weeks is full term, and formally, a pregnancy is only overdue once it reaches 42 weeks. Around 5 in every 100 babies get there. Going past your due date is more likely if it's your first birth, you're over 30, you've gone overdue before or it runs in your family.

## Why they keep a closer eye

If a pregnancy goes overdue, there's a risk the placenta won't supply the baby with enough oxygen and nutrients. The risk of stillbirth or newborn death rises the longer it goes on, though it's still low in Australia. There may also be a higher chance of a large baby, a long labour, breathing problems for the baby, a stay in special care, or a caesarean.

## What you'll be offered

Your midwife or doctor should explain each option, with its risks and benefits, and you work out together what's best. It's your choice whether to have any of it.

- **Monitoring.** Better Health Channel says that if you've gone a week past your due date with no signs of labour, your doctor will want to monitor you closely. Checks may include an ultrasound and a CTG of the baby's heartbeat, perhaps twice a week. They tell you how the baby is at that moment; they can't predict whether that might change. If the baby is well and so are you, you might choose to wait and see whether labour starts.
- **A membrane sweep** (a "stretch and sweep"): during a vaginal examination, your midwife or doctor sweeps a finger around the inside of your cervix, which can trigger hormones that start labour.
- **An induction**, if labour doesn't start on its own, or if the checks show the baby's health is at risk. The sources give the timing loosely: Better Health Channel says generally once a baby is 2 weeks past its due date; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby gives 41 to 42 weeks; the Royal Women's 10 to 12 days past. Ask your own hospital what it does.

## The things people suggest

Nipple stimulation, castor oil, walking, sex, spicy food, hypnosis and acupuncture have all been linked to bringing on labour. The evidence is anecdotal: there isn't enough to show they work. Talk to your doctor or midwife before trying any of them.

## Questions to ask

- Are there any risks to me or the baby if I'm overdue?
- When, where and how would my labour be induced, and are there alternatives?
- Can I have written information on the options?
- How long can I take to think about it?

## When to get help

Pay close attention to the baby's movements. Tell your midwife or doctor straight away if you notice any change: movements that suddenly speed up, slow down or stop.
