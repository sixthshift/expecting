Some vaccines contain a tiny amount of live, weakened virus or bacteria. Because of a very small chance that the live vaccine could pose a risk to the baby, they aren't given in pregnancy. Before any vaccination, tell the doctor or nurse if you may be pregnant.

## Not given in pregnancy

The Australian Immunisation Handbook lists these as **contraindicated** in pregnancy:

- MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) and MMRV (with chickenpox)
- varicella (chickenpox)
- rotavirus (not registered or recommended for adults anyway)
- BCG (tuberculosis)
- oral typhoid (Vivotif oral): the injected typhoid vaccine is used instead
- live oral cholera (Vaxchora): the inactivated oral cholera vaccine is used instead
- Japanese encephalitis (Imojev): the inactivated vaccine (JEspect) is used instead

Two more are **not recommended**:

- **the nasal spray flu vaccine.** The injected flu vaccine is the one to have in pregnancy, though there's no evidence the spray causes harm.
- **yellow fever.** Pregnant people are advised not to go to rural areas where yellow fever is common. If travel to a country with a risk of yellow fever is unavoidable, the Handbook's table says pregnant people should receive the vaccine, and its guidance says to weigh the risks and benefits of vaccination.

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby also says the HPV vaccine isn't recommended in pregnancy: if you started the course, finish it after the birth.

## Before you conceive

Check you're up to date before trying, and have any live vaccines at least 28 days (4 weeks) before you get pregnant. You can see your vaccination history on your immunisation history statement in myGov or My Health Record, or ask your doctor. A blood test can check your immunity to some diseases.

## If you had one by accident

The risk to the baby is theoretical. Pregnant people have had the rubella vaccine, usually without knowing they were pregnant, without harm to the baby, and babies of people given the chickenpox vaccine in early pregnancy haven't had the birth defects it was feared to cause. The Handbook says you don't need to consider ending the pregnancy and should be reassured.

## Not immune to rubella?

Everyone who's pregnant is recommended to have a rubella immunity test. If you're not immune, you'll be offered the vaccine as soon as possible after the birth. The Handbook says vaccination is never contraindicated while breastfeeding.

## Travel vaccines

Most non-live vaccines aren't routinely given in pregnancy, as a precaution, but pregnancy doesn't rule them out. The Handbook recommends them when the benefit of protection outweighs the risk, for example some vaccines before travel to places where a disease is common.

## Your household

It's safe for the people you live with to have live vaccines, including MMR and chickenpox, while you're pregnant.
