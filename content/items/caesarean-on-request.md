Some people would rather have a caesarean than a vaginal birth, for reasons that aren't medical: a previous birth, fear of pain, labour or birth, or other life experiences. It's called a caesarean on maternal request. You can ask, and your doctor or midwife will explain the benefits and risks of every option so you can make an informed choice.

## The options

- **A planned vaginal birth**, with the chance of needing forceps, a vacuum or an emergency caesarean along the way.
- **A planned caesarean**, booked ahead. RANZCOG says one without other risks shouldn't be done before 39 weeks.

RANZCOG's guideline for obstetricians (2023) says that if, after a full discussion over time of the benefits and harms of both, you still ask for a caesarean, the obstetrician may agree to do it, or refer you to a second obstetrician to talk it through and reach a decision. They should respect your request as valid and explore the reasons for it; a thorough talk about fears of pain, labour or birth may ease them. An obstetrician can also decline if they think the risks outweigh the benefits for you, and then either of you can seek a second opinion.

## Things to weigh

RANZCOG's guideline compares planned caesarean with planned vaginal birth. The evidence is low or very low quality, and shows links rather than proven causes; the extra risks are often small in absolute terms.

**A planned caesarean may increase:**

- wound infection and uterine infection, both common
- rarer events: a hysterectomy around the birth, or the death of the birthing parent or baby
- in future pregnancies, the risk of the uterus tearing (rupture) in labour and of placenta accreta, a placenta that grows too deep; these rise with each caesarean
- later surgery for adhesions or an abdominal hernia
- for the child, hospital stays for chest or gut infections in the first year, and asthma

**A planned caesarean may lower:**

- anal sphincter injury, which comes with vaginal birth (more with forceps or a vacuum)
- urinary incontinence a year on, and faecal incontinence compared with an assisted birth
- prolapse and surgery for prolapse or stress incontinence many years later

RANZCOG's patient leaflet adds that some risks may not show until later pregnancies, so think about the size of the family you want. A caesarean is major surgery with a slower recovery.

**Access varies.** The guideline notes that services differ in their capacity to offer it, and decisions take local policies into account. Ask your hospital early how it handles requests.

## When to decide

No source gives a cut-off week. RANZCOG describes the discussion as one that happens over time, and a planned caesarean is usually booked for 39 weeks or later, so raise it early in your care to leave room for the conversation, a second opinion if you want one, and booking.
