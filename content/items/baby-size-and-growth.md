Every baby grows at their own pace, so any size for a given week is a rough average. Babies are born in all different shapes and sizes.

## Rough sizes

From the Better Health Channel:

| Week | About |
|---|---|
| 6 | 3 mm long |
| 8 | 1.3 cm |
| 16 | 14 cm |
| 20 | 21 cm |
| 24 | 33 cm |
| 28 | 1 kg; 25 cm crown to rump, 37 cm crown to toe |
| 36 | 46 cm |
| 40 | 51 cm |

At 40 weeks, a well-grown term baby can weigh anything from 2.9 kg to 4.2 kg. The fruit comparisons in each week's note are a picture, not a measurement.

## How size is measured

**On an ultrasound.** Early in pregnancy, usually before 13 weeks, the sonographer measures from your baby's head to bottom: the crown–rump length. It's compared with population data to date your pregnancy. Later, they measure your baby's head, thigh bone and abdomen to estimate size.

**By measuring your bump.** Fundal height is the distance from your pubic bone to the top of your uterus. Your doctor or midwife may measure it at each visit from 24 weeks. In centimetres it should be about the number of weeks you're pregnant: at 20 weeks, about 20 cm. Smaller than expected could mean your baby is growing slowly or there's too little amniotic fluid; larger could mean your baby is larger than average. Either way, your doctor will monitor you and your baby carefully.

## When growth is slow

The placenta delivers oxygen and nutrients to your baby. If it doesn't work properly (placental insufficiency, uncommon but serious), your baby may not grow as expected: fetal growth restriction. It's often picked up at routine visits or on a routine ultrasound. Your doctor will then watch your baby closely, which might mean extra scans, including a Doppler check of blood flow in the umbilical cord.

You might notice your tummy isn't growing as expected, or is smaller than in a previous pregnancy. If you're worried, speak with your doctor or midwife.

## Birthweight words

- **Low birthweight:** under 2,500 g.
- **Healthy birthweight:** 2,500 to 4,499 g.
- **High birthweight:** 4,500 g or more.

## When to get help

If you're concerned about your baby's movement pattern or notice a decrease in their movements, contact your midwife or doctor immediately. Don't wait until the next day. See [The baby is moving less](baby-moving-less).
