Thyroid hormone matters more in pregnancy: your levels rise to support you and your baby, and until 18 to 20 weeks your baby relies on your supply for their brain development. Thyroid problems affect at least 1 in 25 pregnancies.

## If you already have a thyroid condition

See your doctor to check your thyroid while you're planning a pregnancy and again once you're pregnant. They'll tell you how often you need a check-up. Through the pregnancy you should have regular blood tests to keep your thyroid hormone in the recommended range.

- **Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism).** If you were taking thyroxine before pregnancy, you may need a higher dose once you're pregnant. Ask your doctor how much to take. After the birth you can usually go back to your pre-pregnancy dose.
- **Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism).** Your doctor or specialist may prescribe an anti-thyroid medicine such as propylthiouracil or carbimazole.

If your hypothyroidism is treated effectively, it will not cause problems for you or your baby. Low thyroid hormone levels can cause complications such as premature birth, low birth weight, miscarriage, placental abruption or pre-eclampsia, and may affect the baby's brain development. An overactive thyroid may cause complications such as miscarriage or stillbirth, low birth weight, preterm labour or pre-eclampsia.

## Who gets tested

Your doctor will refer you for a thyroid blood test in pregnancy if you have symptoms, a known thyroid condition or type 1 diabetes. They might also test you if you've had a miscarriage, premature birth or infertility, are over 30, or have a family history of thyroid problems.

## Iodine

Australian guidelines recommend a supplement with 150 micrograms of iodine a day when you're planning a pregnancy, pregnant or breastfeeding; many pregnancy multivitamins contain it. Ask your pharmacist whether any of your medicines could affect your iodine levels or thyroid.

## After the birth

About 1 in 25 people develop postpartum thyroiditis: the thyroid is overactive for a short time, then underactive, then back to normal within a year.
