It's not the cheeriest job on the list, but it's a short one, and a baby is exactly the change it's meant for. Moneysmart says everyone over 18 should have a will, and to update it when your situation changes, naming having children as one of those times.

## The will

A will says what you want to happen to your assets when you die, and it can say **who will look after your children if they're under 18**. It can also set up trusts, such as a testamentary trust, which sits inside the will and holds assets for a child until they reach an age you choose.

If you die without a will, called dying intestate, the government decides who gets your assets. That can take longer, and may not be what you wanted.

## Making one

- **A solicitor** prepares it for a fee.
- **Your state or territory's Public Trustee** prepares wills at no cost for some people, such as pensioners, people over 60, or people who appoint the Public Trustee as executor. Eligibility differs by state, so check your Public Trustee's website.
- **Low-cost options:** The Salvation Army's community wills days, or a will kit. If you use an online will kit, have a solicitor or Public Trustee check it: a will not done properly may be invalid.

Keep it somewhere safe, such as a fireproof safe, or with your solicitor, the Courts or the Public Trustee, and tell someone you trust where it is.

## Super and its insurance sit outside the will

Your will doesn't automatically cover your super, or life insurance held through it. You nominate beneficiaries with each fund: your dependants, or your legal personal representative (such as your executor) if you want it paid through your will. Without a valid binding nomination, the fund's trustee decides who gets it. Binding nominations generally expire every 3 years, so keep yours up to date.

ASIC found almost 60% of super members hadn't nominated a beneficiary at all, and only 10% had a binding nomination.

## If you can't decide for yourself

An estate plan can also cover your medical care and finances if you can no longer make your own decisions:

- an **enduring power of attorney**, for someone to make financial and legal decisions if you lose the ability to
- an **enduring guardian** (sometimes called a medical power of attorney), for medical and lifestyle decisions

Once you lose capacity, you can't put these in place, and someone may need to apply to act for you. Each state and territory has its own rules.
