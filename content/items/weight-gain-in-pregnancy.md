As your baby grows, you'll usually put on weight gradually. How much depends on what you weighed before pregnancy, among other things. The ranges below are a guide only: your midwife, doctor or dietitian may recommend something different for you.

## Where the weight goes

Some of it is your baby. Your body also builds extra tissue and holds more fluid, including the amniotic fluid around your baby, the placenta and a larger volume of blood.

## The ranges

Both sources give the same ranges, based on your BMI before pregnancy. The Royal Women's Hospital gives lower BMI cut-offs if you're of Asian background (in brackets).

| Pre-pregnancy BMI | Total gain | Per month, 2nd and 3rd trimesters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 18.5 | 12.5 to 18 kg | 2 to 2.6 kg |
| 18.5 to 24.9 (18.5 to 22.9 if Asian) | 11.5 to 16 kg | 1.5 to 2.3 kg |
| 25 to 29.9 (23 to 27.5 if Asian) | 7 to 11.5 kg | 1 to 1.5 kg |
| 30 or more (over 27.5 if Asian) | 5 to 9 kg (under 7 kg if Asian) | 0.8 to 1.2 kg |

In the first three months, the Royal Women's says the average gain is ½ to 2 kg for everyone. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby notes BMI doesn't necessarily show your health, or how fat is spread around your body.

## What changes it

- **Morning sickness.** Some people lose weight early on because of it, and severe nausea is one of the things a professional will factor in.
- **Twins or more.** You may gain more if you're having more than one baby.
- How and what you eat, how active you are, changes in appetite such as cravings and aversions, and fluid retention, especially later in pregnancy.

## How it's tracked

Your weight gain is monitored at your antenatal checkups. If you'd like to follow it yourself, the Royal Women's has worksheets for each BMI range. If you're concerned about your weight gain, talk to your doctor or midwife.

## Why it matters

Gaining more than recommended increases the risk of complications such as gestational diabetes, high blood pressure, pre-eclampsia, caesarean section and stillbirth, and babies are more likely to be born early and need intensive care. Gaining less than recommended, or starting from a low weight, makes it more likely your baby is born early or small.

You don't need to "eat for 2". You probably won't need many extra kilojoules in the first three months; in the second and third trimesters, a bit more vegetables, lean protein and wholegrains each day is enough.
