Most people having twins have a healthy pregnancy and healthy babies. But there are more risks than with one baby, so you'll be offered more checks along the way.

## Two kinds of twins

- **Fraternal (dizygotic, non-identical)** twins come from two eggs fertilised by two sperm. They each have their own placenta and their own sac, may or may not be the same sex, and look alike only as any siblings might.
- **Identical (monozygotic)** twins come from one fertilised egg that splits in two. They have the same genes and are always the same sex. They may share a placenta or each have their own.

## Sharing a placenta or a sac

- **DCDA (dichorionic diamniotic):** each baby has its own placenta and its own sac. This is the most common type of twin pregnancy.
- **MCDA (monochorionic diamniotic):** the babies share a placenta but each has its own sac.
- **MCMA (monochorionic monoamniotic):** the babies share a placenta and a sac. This is rarer.

Triplets or more can share in the same ways. An ultrasound can show whether your babies are fraternal or identical, and whether they share a placenta. It's very important to find out, because shared-placenta pregnancies need careful monitoring. Some complications only happen in twins who share a placenta, such as twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), where blood flows from one twin to the other. TTTS is rare, and most identical twins won't get it. With MCMA twins, the umbilical cords can become tangled, which can put both babies' lives at risk.

## What's different for you

Twin pregnancy can be more tiring, physically and emotionally. You're more likely to have backache, heartburn, morning sickness, swollen ankles, tiredness and varicose veins. Some problems are also more common: anaemia, gestational diabetes, high blood pressure and pre-eclampsia, severe vomiting (hyperemesis gravidarum) and miscarriage. Twins are more likely to be born early (before 37 weeks) and small, so your team may recommend a hospital that can care for twins, and may advise against a home birth or birth centre.

Eat well, drink plenty, and ask about supplements such as folate, iodine and iron. How much weight to gain depends on you; talk it over with your doctor, midwife or dietitian.

## A vanishing twin

Sometimes an early ultrasound shows twins, and by the next scan one can no longer be seen, because that twin miscarried early. Most of the time it causes no problems for the other twin.

## Support

The Australian Multiple Birth Association and Twins Research Australia have information and connect you with other twin families.

## When to get help

Contact your doctor immediately if your tummy feels tight, is painful or suddenly gets bigger, if you put on weight very quickly, or if you're short of breath. These could be signs of TTTS.
