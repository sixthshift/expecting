Your abdominal muscles support your spine and organs and give you core stability. In pregnancy the growing baby and uterus stretch and weaken them, and pregnancy hormones soften your ligaments and abdominal muscles, so they work less well at supporting and moving your body.

## Abdominal separation

The two long muscles down the middle of your tummy (the rectus abdominis, the "six-pack" muscles) can separate. It's called abdominal separation, diastasis recti or DRAM. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says it's common, may happen in the later stages of pregnancy, and is a natural adaptation to your growing baby. It may be more likely if you've had more than one pregnancy or are overweight.

You may see a gap, and you may feel your core is weak, for example lifting a laundry basket. It isn't painful, but you may get lower back pain. To check: lie on your back with knees bent, put flat fingertips across your tummy at belly button level, lift your head and shoulders and feel for the muscle edges coming together; a gap wider than one finger may be a separation. If you think you have it, see your doctor, midwife or maternal health nurse. They can measure it with fingers, a tape measure or an ultrasound, and may refer you to a women's health physiotherapist for an exercise program. Occasionally surgery is recommended.

## What to do

- **Skip sit-ups and crunches.** They're not recommended in pregnancy: they're less effective on stretched muscles, and done lying on your back the baby's weight rests on major blood vessels, which can cause dizziness.
- **Draw in instead.** The Royal Women's Hospital recommends drawing your abdominal muscles in without moving your spine, as if sucking your belly button towards your backbone. On hands and knees, relax your tummy, breathe in, then breathe out and gently draw in the lower tummy, lifting your baby. Hold for a count of three or more, up to 10 times, building up gradually.
- **Group classes.** It suggests clinical pilates, a fitball class, boxercise, aquarobics or a "Pump" class with light weights.
- **Support.** An abdominal support band or firm compression underwear can help.

## After the birth

Separation often gets better in the weeks and months after the birth, though up to 1 in 3 people still report it a year later. To stop it getting worse: don't lift things heavier than your baby, roll onto your side to get out of bed or sit up, and skip sit-ups, oblique curls and some yoga poses.
