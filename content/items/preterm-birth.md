A baby born before 37 weeks is premature, or preterm. In Australia about 8 in every 100 babies are. Often the cause isn't known. Most babies born between 32 and 37 weeks don't have serious long-term problems; babies born before 32 weeks have a higher risk of health and developmental problems.

## When to get help

**If you're less than 37 weeks pregnant and have any sign of labour, contact your midwife, doctor or nearest birthing centre immediately.** The signs include:

- contractions
- your waters breaking
- mucus, blood or fluid leaking from your vagina
- pressure in your pelvis
- cramping or pain in your lower tummy
- lower back pain

It may be possible to slow or stop labour, and every day the baby stays inside is likely to be better for their health.

## What the hospital does

You may need to go to a hospital with a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or special care nursery (SCN). There, your doctor or midwife will ask about your symptoms, examine you, probably do a pelvic examination or ultrasound, check whether your cervix is opening, and monitor you and the baby. What happens next depends on your health, the baby's health and how many weeks you are. You may be given:

- medicines to try to delay the birth
- corticosteroid injections, to lower the baby's risk of complications such as breathing difficulties
- if your waters have broken early, you're under 30 weeks and the birth is expected within 24 hours, a magnesium sulphate drip to help protect the baby's brain

## The birth

Premature babies can be born very quickly. Sometimes your doctor will recommend a caesarean as the safest way, and talk through the risks and benefits with you. A team from the newborn unit is there for the birth and takes over the baby's care straight away. Once the baby is stable they may go to the NICU or SCN, which may mean a transfer to another hospital if yours has no NICU.

## The baby's path

Babies born between 32 and 37 weeks may need care in a special care nursery. Many born before 32 weeks need help breathing and usually start in a NICU. Almost 1 in 5 babies in Australia is admitted to one or the other.

How soon you can hold the baby depends on how they are; staff will help you hold them skin-to-skin as soon as you can. The baby may be fed breastmilk through a tube at first, and you may be shown how to express. In the SCN you can visit as often as you like, and parents are treated as part of the care team. You may go home before your baby does.

It's a stressful time, and many parents feel distant from their baby or anxious about the future. Talk to the nursery staff, who can arrange a social worker, and lean on family, friends and other parents who've been there.
