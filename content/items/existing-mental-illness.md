If you live with a mental illness, talk to your doctor or midwife early, ideally before you start trying and otherwise as soon as you're pregnant. Pregnancy can affect your mental health, and your mental health can affect the pregnancy. Planning ahead helps you stay as well as possible.

## Planning your care

Your healthcare team will listen to what matters to you, including your culture, your community and your ways of staying strong. Depending on your needs and what's available locally, they may refer you to a perinatal mental health team, who specialise in mental illness in pregnancy and after the birth and will plan treatment with your wishes, cultural background and health needs in mind. Being supported by the same team throughout can reduce stress and improve outcomes for you and your baby.

Living with a mental illness can bring a higher risk of some complications, such as gestational diabetes, a baby born small or early, and miscarriage or stillbirth. Different conditions affect pregnancy in different ways, and your doctor will talk these through with you.

## Your medicines

If you take medicines every day, **do not stop or change your dose without first discussing it with your doctor.** Some are safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding, including most SSRIs for depression and anxiety. Others need to be changed: valproic acid, used for bipolar disorder, is not recommended in pregnancy. Your doctor will go through the risks and benefits of each option, including how stopping might affect your mental health, so you can decide together.

## Particular conditions

**Bipolar disorder.** For some, giving birth triggers a first bipolar episode. If you've already been diagnosed, the Royal Women's advises planning the pregnancy with a doctor and never stopping medication abruptly. You're at greatest risk of relapse in the first month after the birth, so extra monitoring then is a good idea. Bipolar disorder also raises the risk of postpartum psychosis.

**Eating disorders.** Pregnancy can be tricky because you have less control over your eating and weight gain, and some people find that very distressing. You're likely to need extra monitoring, ideally from a team that may include a dietitian, a physician and a psychiatrist as well as your midwife and obstetrician. Relapse is a risk after the birth: if you find yourself dieting, purging or exercising excessively again, go back to your health professionals.

**ADHD.** Pregnancy adds appointments, planning and memory load, hormones and medication changes can shift symptoms, and ADHD raises the risk of depression and anxiety after the birth. Gidget suggests apps and reminders for appointments, and taking notes or a support person to appointments.

## For the partner

The Royal Women's describes treatment for bipolar disorder as a shared process between you, your partner and your GP or psychiatrist.

## When to see your doctor

- before you start trying for a baby
- during pregnancy if you, or people around you, are worried you're not acting like your usual self
- towards the end of pregnancy, to plan your mental health care after the birth and while breastfeeding

If you're not coping, speak with your perinatal mental health team, midwife, GP or obstetrician. To talk to someone, call Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636.
