It may seem early to read this before the baby comes. But signs of postnatal depression are often missed or dismissed as a normal part of life with a new baby, so knowing them now, both of you, makes them easier to spot later.

## The baby blues

Very common: about 4 in every 5 people who give birth have them, usually 3 to 5 days after the birth. You might feel tearful, sensitive, anxious or overwhelmed, moody or irritable, and find it hard to sleep even when the baby is settled. Changing hormones may play a part. The blues usually pass quickly on their own, without treatment.

What helps: accepting practical help and emotional support, talking to the people close to you, eating well, a little exercise, and as much rest as you can get.

## Postnatal depression and anxiety

These are different from the blues. Postnatal depression lasts longer than 2 weeks, may be more severe, and usually gets in the way of daily life and of caring for yourself or the baby. It doesn't usually go away on its own. Signs include feeling low, numb or hopeless, losing interest in others including the baby, changes in sleep or appetite, and thoughts of harming yourself or the baby.

Postnatal anxiety is constant worry that affects everyday life: fear that something bad will happen to the baby, trouble sleeping, finding it hard to concentrate or make decisions. Many people have both.

Both are common and treatable, and there's no need to feel ashamed. Your doctor will routinely check how you're feeling in the months after the birth, often with a questionnaire called the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale. Try to answer honestly.

## For the partner

If the person who gave birth doesn't seem like themselves, check in gently. You don't need perfect words: "I've noticed that you don't seem like yourself. How have you been feeling lately?" Listen without rushing to fix things. If they seem very withdrawn, anxious or overwhelmed, or are struggling to cope day to day, encourage them to speak with their GP, midwife or a mental health clinician. The PANDA helpline is for support people too, so you can call it yourself.

## When to get help

**Call 000** if you, or someone you know, is having suicidal thoughts and is in immediate danger. For help and support, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or PANDA on 1300 726 306. If you're having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or call triple zero (000) for immediate support. To talk to someone now, you can also call the Suicide Call Back Service on 1300 659 467.

**See your doctor, midwife or child health nurse** if you're not feeling better after 2 weeks, or you're not sure whether what you feel is normal. You can also call PANDA on 1300 726 306 or Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636.
