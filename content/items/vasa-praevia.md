Vasa praevia is when blood vessels belonging to the baby, from the umbilical cord or the placenta, run unprotected through the membranes, across or near the cervix. As the cervix opens in labour, or when the waters break, these vessels can tear. If they do, it's the baby who loses blood, not the parent, and that is an emergency.

It's rare. An Australian study found about 1 in every 4,700 pregnancies; worldwide reviews put it nearer 1 in 1,300. Found before labour, with a plan for the birth, most babies are born healthy: RANZCOG's guideline gives a survival rate of about 99%.

## What raises the chance

It isn't clear why it happens, and nothing prevents it. It's more likely if:

- the placenta is low-lying, or was low and has since moved up
- the umbilical cord joins the membranes instead of the placenta (velamentous cord insertion)
- the placenta has two or more lobes (bilobed, multilobed or succenturiate)
- the pregnancy came from IVF or other assisted conception
- you're having twins or more

Having it in an earlier pregnancy doesn't seem to raise your chance of having it again.

## The scans

RANZCOG suggests every pregnancy be checked for vasa praevia at the second trimester anatomy scan, at about 18 to 20 weeks, when the placenta and cord can be seen.

If that scan shows a low-lying placenta, the cord joining the membranes, a placenta with more than one lobe, or the baby's vessels within 5 cm of the cervix, you'll be offered another scan at about 28 to 32 weeks. It's usually a transvaginal (internal) scan, the most accurate way to tell. Many early findings change as the placenta grows, which is why the later scan decides. At that scan:

- **vessels within 2 cm of the cervix** mean vasa praevia
- **vessels 2 to 5 cm away** are low-lying fetal vessels, not vasa praevia; you can usually carry on with your own care and birth plan, though any bleeding usually means being admitted to hospital
- **vessels more than 5 cm away** mean you don't have it, and need no more scans for it

## If you have vasa praevia

You'll usually see a senior obstetrician. It's likely that:

- **you'll talk through staying at home or in hospital** until the birth, from about 32 weeks. Part of that is how quickly you could reach a hospital that can do an emergency caesarean and care for a newborn; RANZCOG's guideline uses 30 minutes. If your local hospital can't, you'll most likely need to move to a hospital in a larger city for the rest of the pregnancy.
- **the birth will be a planned caesarean, before labour starts** or the waters break. RANZCOG suggests from 36 weeks, or from 34 weeks if you have other risks such as a short cervix, twins, an earlier preterm birth or vaginal bleeding.
- **if you're at home,** you may be advised to avoid vigorous exercise and vaginal sex.

Babies born early may need extra care for their first weeks; you can ask to talk with a paediatrician beforehand. The diagnosis can be worrying, and your midwife or doctor can help you find support, such as counselling.

## When to get help

If you have vasa praevia, or risk factors for it, **go to the hospital immediately** if you have:

- labour pains or contractions
- any vaginal bleeding
- your waters breaking
- a change in the baby's movements, including moving less or not at all
