Amniocentesis is a diagnostic test, done from 15 weeks, that can confirm whether your baby has a genetic condition or a difference in their chromosomes. Your doctor or midwife may recommend it, but it's your choice whether to have it. You may be referred to a genetic counsellor to help you understand the test and what the results may mean.

**Why it might be offered.** After a screening result suggesting a higher than expected chance of a genetic difference, if you already have a child with one, or if both parents are known carriers of a genetic condition. It can also be used to diagnose a neural tube problem, check for anaemia or lung maturity, find the baby's blood type, or look for an infection such as cytomegalovirus or toxoplasmosis.

**Where, and the cost.** Some hospitals and specialist ultrasound clinics, and only an obstetrician with specific training does it. Medicare usually covers part of the cost; with a private obstetrician there may be consultation or procedure fees. Ask before you book.

**What happens.** An ultrasound first checks the baby and the placenta. The doctor then passes a fine needle through your tummy into the uterus, guided by ultrasound the whole time, and takes about 15 to 30 mL of fluid. It usually takes a few minutes. You may feel mild discomfort, and you may be offered a local anaesthetic. If you have a negative blood group, you'll be given an anti-D injection. You can have someone with you.

**Afterwards.** Mild cramping, some spotting or a few drops of fluid from the vagina can happen. Rarely there are complications: leaking fluid (usually a small amount that stops by itself within a week), your waters breaking, infection, injury to the baby, or miscarriage. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby calls the miscarriage risk very small.

**Seek urgent medical attention** if after an amniocentesis you have ongoing loss of fluid or bleeding, severe cramping for a few hours, or a fever: contact your maternity care provider immediately or go to your nearest emergency department.
