An episiotomy is a cut made in your perineum (the tissue between your vagina and anus) during birth, to widen the vaginal opening and make more room for the baby. It's not recommended for every vaginal birth, and it may only be done with your consent.

## Why it might be recommended

- during an assisted birth (forceps or a vacuum cup), to prevent severe tearing or trauma
- because the baby is distressed and needs to be born quickly
- because a severe tear looks likely

In those situations it can lower your risk of a severe tear, though you may still tear as well.

## How it's done

If you don't have an epidural, or it isn't working well, you're given a local anaesthetic injection to numb the area first. The cut is made with surgical scissors. After the birth it's stitched with dissolvable stitches, which don't need removing. You'll be offered ice packs and pain relief for swelling and soreness.

## If you'd rather not have one

Talk about it with your midwife or doctor before labour, and put it in your birth preferences; your team will do their best to support them. If something unexpected happens and one is recommended, they'll explain the risks and benefits, and you make the final decision. It's also worth asking what your hospital does to lower the chance of a serious tear.

Perineal massage, usually started at about 35 weeks if you're planning a vaginal birth, may reduce your chance of needing an episiotomy. Don't do it if you have a short cervix, a low-lying placenta, bleeding, severe blood pressure problems, an active genital infection such as herpes or thrush, or your waters have broken.

## Recovery

The stitches usually dissolve in 1 to 2 weeks, and it may take up to a month to fully recover. Things that help:

- rest, lying on your side if you can, and change pads often
- shower daily and after a poo, and pat dry; no creams or powders unless you've been told to use them
- drink plenty of water, eat plenty of fibre, and don't strain on the toilet
- pour water over the area or lean forward when you wee, to ease stinging
- start gentle pelvic floor exercises 2 to 3 days after the birth, or when it's comfortable
- avoid sit-ups, lifting and high-impact exercise

Having one doesn't mean you'll always need one next time.

## When to get help

Contact your doctor or midwife if you see signs of infection: swelling, pus or fluid from the scar, ongoing pain or an unusual smell. Most people have a postnatal check at 6 weeks, but see your doctor earlier if you're worried about healing, or if:

- you develop a temperature
- the area becomes inflamed, swollen and red
- it's still painful after 2 to 3 weeks
- you can't control your bowels or wind
- sex is painful
- you have a heavy, dragging feeling in your vagina
