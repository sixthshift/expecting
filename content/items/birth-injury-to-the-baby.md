Having a baby in Australia is very safe, but about 1 in every 200 babies born in Australian hospitals has a birth injury (sometimes called neonatal birth trauma). Most are temporary and get better on their own. A birth injury is never due to anything you did or didn't do.

## What makes them more likely

The baby not getting enough oxygen in labour (fetal distress), a vacuum or forceps birth, the baby in an unusual position, or a shoulder getting stuck (shoulder dystocia), especially with a large baby. Sometimes an injury comes from a life-saving procedure.

## The kinds

- **Scalp swelling and bruising** are the most common: soft swelling from the trip through the birth canal (caput succedaneum) or from a vacuum cup (a chignon). It usually goes in a few days.
- **A cephalhaematoma** is bleeding between the skull bone and its covering, a raised lump that may appear a few hours after birth. Depending on its size it takes 2 weeks to 3 months to go.
- **Broken bones**, most often the collarbone, sometimes the upper arm, more likely after shoulder dystocia.
- **Nerve damage.** Forceps occasionally press on the facial nerve. The nerves running from the neck down the arm (the brachial plexus) can be stretched, weakening the arm; Erb's palsy is the most common. Nerve damage in a newborn is usually temporary.
- **Brain injury** is very rare, and can happen if the baby doesn't get enough oxygen in labour. **Brain bleeds** are rare, and more common in a very premature baby.

## What's checked and done

A routine check usually picks up nerve problems while you're still in hospital, and the team watches the baby's movement improve over time, sometimes with physiotherapy. Soft-tissue injuries normally need no treatment beyond watching. A broken bone may need an x-ray, and the arm or leg held still with supports or splints.

## For you

It can be very distressing to watch your baby recover, and birth can feel traumatic for either parent. If yours did, it was. Ask your doctor or midwife for a debrief to understand what happened; some hospitals offer one formally. If you're worried about how you're coping, it's never too late to ask your doctor, midwife or child health nurse for help.
