Shoulder dystocia is when one of the baby's shoulders gets stuck during birth, behind your pubic bone or your sacrum (the bone at the back of your pelvis, above your tailbone). It happens in about 1 in every 100 vaginal births, and the baby needs urgent help to be born.

## How it shows

In the second stage of labour there's normally a pause after the baby's head is born, before the body comes out. With shoulder dystocia that delay is longer than normal, and your midwife or doctor will look for other signs too.

## Who it's more likely for

It's impossible to predict, but it's more likely if you:

- had shoulder dystocia in a previous labour
- had your labour induced, or other interventions during labour
- are having a large baby (fetal macrosomia)
- are living with overweight
- have diabetes

It can also happen if labour goes very slowly. If you've had it before, your doctor or midwife will talk with you about the risk and how to manage it.

## What the team does

Shoulder dystocia is a medical emergency: while the baby is stuck they can't breathe, and the cord may be squeezed. Your midwife or doctor will first tell you to **stop pushing**, and call for help.

- **The McRoberts position.** Often a change of position frees the shoulder: lying flat on your back with your knees pulled back as far as they go. Most shoulder dystocias are solved with this.
- **Pressure above the pubic bone**, pressed gently to free the shoulder.
- **All fours**, on your hands and knees.
- **A hand inside.** Sometimes they need to put their fingers into the vagina to free the baby's body, and you may need an episiotomy first to make room. They'll explain and ask for your consent.

In rare cases they need to break the baby's collarbone to get them out (it heals quickly), or do an emergency caesarean, sometimes under a general anaesthetic.

## After the birth

The baby may be watched more closely or spend time in the neonatal intensive care unit, and some need physiotherapy. Injuries can include a broken collarbone or upper arm, or nerve damage affecting the shoulder, arm, hand or fingers. Sadly, lack of oxygen can lead to brain damage or even death. Most babies recover very well.

For you, it can lead to tears, nerve damage or heavy bleeding (postpartum haemorrhage). These are rare, and doctors and midwives are trained to deal with them.

Feeling shocked, guilty or worried is common. Your doctor or midwife will talk with you about why it might have happened, and can help with the feelings too. An upright position for the birth (sitting, standing, kneeling, squatting or on all fours) may lower the chance of it.
