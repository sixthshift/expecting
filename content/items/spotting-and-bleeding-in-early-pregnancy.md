## What's usual

Bleeding in early pregnancy is common. The Royal Women's Hospital says about 1 in 4 pregnant people bleed early on, and many go on to have a healthy baby. Light spotting doesn't always mean a miscarriage.

It can have several causes: implantation bleeding (light spotting as the fertilised egg attaches to the lining of the uterus), a miscarriage, an ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy growing outside the uterus, which can be life-threatening), or, rarely, a molar pregnancy.

It helps to note how much you're bleeding, the colour (bright red or dark brown), whether you've passed clots and whether you have pain. If you can, keep your pads or stained clothes to show your doctor or midwife.

## Call 000 or go to emergency

Call triple zero (000) or go to your nearest emergency department immediately if you have:

- heavy bleeding: soaking 1 or more pads in an hour, or bleeding you can't catch or contain, or large clots
- severe pain in your belly
- pain in the tip of your shoulder
- smelly vaginal discharge
- a fever
- feeling faint, dizzy or unwell, or trouble breathing

Go to your nearest emergency department immediately if you have chills or a fast heartbeat with bleeding.

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby's bleeding page counts 2 pads an hour as heavy; its miscarriage page says 1. Go by 1.

## Contact your hospital now

Contact your doctor or midwife for spotting or bleeding at any stage of pregnancy. If the bleeding isn't heavy and you feel well, contact them straight away to find out what to do; they may refer you to hospital or an early pregnancy assessment service, or arrange a blood test and an ultrasound. If you haven't started pregnancy care yet, call your GP.

If you have a negative blood type and you miscarry, check with your doctor whether you need a dose of anti-D.
