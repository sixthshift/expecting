The choice to have any test, scan or treatment in pregnancy is yours. Your rights are set out in the Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights, and they apply everywhere: public and private hospitals, general practice and the community.

## Informed consent

Consent is your agreement to a test, medicine, treatment or procedure. For it to be informed, your doctor or midwife should explain why it's recommended, what it involves, and its benefits, risks, costs and alternatives, and you should have the chance to ask questions. It must be voluntary: no one can force you.

Consent can be spoken, written or implied (holding out your arm for a blood pressure check). For surgery you'll usually sign a form. In most cases you can change your mind even after signing; tell your doctor clearly. The one exception is an emergency, when urgent treatment is needed to save your life or prevent serious harm. If there's something you wouldn't want even then, such as a blood transfusion, tell your team in advance.

## Saying no

You can decide to have all the recommended tests, some, or none. You can refuse a treatment, and you can ask for more time to decide. Before you do, make sure you understand the benefits and risks of the treatment, and of doing nothing.

If you disagree with a recommendation, your doctor or midwife must respect your decision and can keep caring for you; you won't need to switch providers. They may check you understand the risks, ask about your reasons, talk through alternatives, and note it in your record and ask you to sign.

## Shared decision making

Your doctor or midwife brings the evidence about each option; you bring your values, goals, culture and circumstances, and you can involve your partner. Useful questions: What are my options? What are the possible benefits and harms? How likely is each for me? A decision aid can help, if one exists for your choice.

## A second opinion, or a complaint

You have a right to a second opinion if you disagree with your doctor or midwife: ask hospital staff how. If you're not satisfied, you can speak to your doctor or midwife, your state's health complaints agency or health department, AHPRA (about a registered practitioner's unsafe behaviour or practice) or the National Health Practitioner Ombudsman. Giving feedback or complaining must not affect your care. After labour, birth or an emergency, you can ask for a debrief.

## Interpreters

TIS National, on 131 450, provides interpreters for health appointments, usually free of charge.

## Your records

My Health Record holds things like scan and blood test reports, medicines and discharge summaries. It can be seen by you, your healthcare providers and anyone you choose to share it with. You can set access controls, see who has looked at it, and delete it at any time.
