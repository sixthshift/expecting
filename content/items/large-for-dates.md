A "big baby" (macrosomia) has no single definition. Queensland's clinical guideline describes it as a birth weight over 4000 to 4500 g at 40 weeks, or above the 90th centile for the estimated weight at that stage of pregnancy.

## How it's picked up

Your midwife or doctor measures your bump at checkups. Estimating a baby's weight by feeling and measuring the bump is imprecise. If the measurement suggests a big baby (for example, 3 cm more than expected from 36 weeks), Queensland's guideline recommends an ultrasound to estimate the baby's weight. It doesn't recommend a weight scan for everyone.

An ultrasound can check your baby's growth and size, and you may be offered extra scans if you have pregnancy complications.

## How accurate the scan is

Ultrasound weight estimates have improved over time. Recent studies show random errors of less than 10%, and accuracy depends on things like the sonographer's skill and the equipment. So the number on the report is an estimate, not a weighing.

## Why diabetes comes up

High blood sugar in pregnancy raises the chance of a large baby (over 4.5 kg), as well as of needing interventions in labour such as a caesarean. Having had a large baby in a previous pregnancy is one of the things that puts you at higher risk of gestational diabetes, so tell your doctor or midwife if you have.

## What it might change

- **More checks.** Pregnancy, Birth and Baby lists differences in growth (being smaller or bigger than normal) among the risks to the baby in a higher-risk pregnancy; a higher-risk pregnancy usually means closer watching: more blood tests, scans or appointments.
- **A conversation about timing.** Queensland's guideline says induction for a suspected big baby based on a hands-on examination alone is not recommended. It suggests discussing induction from 38 weeks if the scan estimate at 36 weeks or later is greater than the 97th centile, or reaches set weights (the guideline lists 3500 g at approximately 36 weeks, 3700 g at approximately 37, 3900 g at approximately 38). It suggests considering a planned caesarean if the estimate is over 4500 g with diabetes, or over 5000 g without. In the research it cites, induction for a suspected big baby lowered the risk of shoulder dystocia (the shoulders getting stuck) and of fractures, didn't change the rate of caesarean or assisted birth, and raised the risk of third and fourth degree tears.
- **In labour,** a baby who is very large for their gestational age is one of the reasons labour can be slow.

Hospitals differ, so ask your own what your scan result means for you.
