Gestational diabetes is diabetes that starts in pregnancy. It affects around 1 in 10 pregnancies. The placenta makes hormones that help the baby grow but also reduce the action of insulin, and if your body can't keep up with the extra insulin it needs, your blood sugar runs high. It often causes no symptoms, which is why everyone is offered the glucose tolerance test at 24 to 28 weeks. It does not mean the baby will be born with diabetes.

Managed well, it lowers the risks for you and the baby. Unmanaged high blood sugar raises the chance of a large baby, high blood pressure, pre-eclampsia, miscarriage or stillbirth, and needing interventions such as a caesarean.

## Your care from here

- **More appointments.** You may need more frequent antenatal visits, and a referral to an obstetrician, a dietitian and a diabetes educator.
- **Finger-prick checks.** You'll check and record your blood glucose at least 4 times a day: on waking, and 1 or 2 hours after each main meal. Your team will teach you how and set your targets.
- **Food.** Three main meals and 2 to 3 snacks spread through the day. A dietitian will give you personal advice.
- **Moving.** Moderate activity, such as walking, swimming or cycling, helps manage blood glucose. Check with your doctor or midwife first.
- **Medicine, sometimes.** If your levels stay high despite diet and activity, insulin is considered safe in pregnancy and metformin also appears to be safe. The risks of not treating gestational diabetes could be much higher than the risks of the medicine.
- **The NDSS.** If you're eligible for Medicare, register with the National Diabetes Services Scheme for subsidised blood glucose strips, insulin devices and needles, usually from community pharmacies.

## The birth

Gestational diabetes doesn't usually seem to affect having a vaginal birth. It's strongly recommended that you give birth with the support of a consultant-led maternity team in a hospital.

## After the birth

The baby is monitored for the first 24 to 48 hours, with a heel-prick glucose check 2 to 4 hours after birth. Feeding within 30 minutes and at least every 3 hours over the next 24 hours helps keep the baby's blood glucose safe; some people express colostrum in late pregnancy in case it's needed. Any medicine for gestational diabetes usually stops straight after the birth.

## Later

Around 1 in 2 people with gestational diabetes develop type 2 diabetes in the future. The Royal Women's says to have another glucose tolerance test when the baby is 6 to 12 weeks old, and Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says to then test for type 2 diabetes every 3 years if you're not planning another pregnancy, and especially if you are.
