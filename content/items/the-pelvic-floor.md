Your pelvic floor is a group of muscles and ligaments that supports your bladder, uterus and bowel. The muscles form a sling that attaches to your pubic bone at the front and your tailbone at the back. The urethra, vagina and anus all pass through it.

## What it does

A strong pelvic floor keeps your organs in place, which prevents prolapse (the bladder, uterus or bowel losing support), and helps you control your bladder and bowel, so you can "hold on" until the right time and place. It also matters for sexual function.

In pregnancy it has an extra job: supporting your growing baby. In labour and birth, the same muscles need to relax and stretch to let your baby be born.

## Why pregnancy matters to it

During pregnancy and childbirth your pelvic floor can become weak, stretched, strained or damaged. Pregnancy and birth (caesarean or vaginal) are both named as things that weaken it, along with constipation and straining, heavy lifting, excessive coughing, sitting for long periods and living with obesity. Birth is harder on it after a large baby, a long time pushing or an assisted birth with forceps or vacuum.

A weak pelvic floor can show as needing to wee often or leaking urine, trouble controlling your bowel, or prolapse symptoms such as pressure or a bulge. Damage can sometimes lead to incontinence during and after pregnancy, ranging from a small leak to a complete loss of control.

## Finding the muscles

Sit or lie with your thighs, buttocks and stomach relaxed. Imagine stopping yourself passing wind, or holding on from passing urine, and squeeze those muscles. You should feel them lift up inside you, around your anus and vagina, and a definite let go when you relax. Keep breathing normally.

If you can't feel a distinct tightening, see a women's health (pelvic floor) physiotherapist or a continence nurse.

## The two exercises (from the Royal Women's Hospital)

1. **Slow holds.** Tighten around your front passage, vagina and back passage as strongly as you can and hold for 3 to 5 seconds, longer if you can but no more than 8 seconds. Rest a few seconds and repeat up to 10 times, or until the muscles tire. If you can, do 3 sets a day in different positions.
2. **Quick squeezes.** Squeeze and lift as strongly and quickly as you can, then let go straight away. Repeat 10 to 20 times, one to three times a day.

Exercise your pelvic floor every day for the rest of your life; it's best to start before pregnancy and keep going while you're pregnant. Link them to something you do daily, like waiting for the kettle, and squeeze your pelvic floor whenever you cough, sneeze or lift.

## After the birth

Pelvic floor function often recovers within the first year. You may be able to restart gentle exercises from 48 hours after the birth, even with perineal trauma; check with your doctor or midwife about your situation. The perineum, and perineal massage from around 34 weeks, have their own entries.

## When to get help

If you have bladder or bowel problems, see your doctor. They may refer you to a physiotherapist or continence nurse.
