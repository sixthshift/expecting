At around 36 weeks, your doctor or midwife feels your tummy to check which way the baby is lying. If they think the baby might be breech, they may send you for an ultrasound to confirm it.

**The words you might hear.**

- **Cephalic** (or vertex): head down, ready for birth. Towards the end of pregnancy most babies are head down and facing your back.
- **Breech**: bottom or feet towards your cervix. Most babies lie breech until late pregnancy, and most turn head down before the last month. About 3 in 100 are still breech at the end of pregnancy.
- **Posterior**: head down but facing your belly, with the back of the baby's head against your spine.
- **Lateral**: the back of the baby's head facing your side.

**If the baby is posterior or lateral.** The Royal Women's Hospital says there isn't a lot of concern even in late pregnancy, since babies usually move into a good position, even during labour. If the baby stays that way, labour may be a little longer, with a bit more back pain; being on your hands and knees may help with the back pain.

**If the baby is breech.** In an uncomplicated pregnancy no extra care is needed until about 37 weeks. After that, your doctor or midwife may suggest turning the baby (an external cephalic version, or ECV), and you'll talk about your options for the birth if the baby stays breech.

**If your waters break** and you've been told the baby isn't head first, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.
