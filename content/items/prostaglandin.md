Prostaglandin is a hormone that gets your body ready for labour. In an induction, a synthetic version is placed in your vagina to ripen the cervix: to soften it and open it. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says it's often the preferred way to induce, because it's the closest to natural labour. Which method you're offered is decided after a vaginal examination to see how ready your cervix is.

## The gel and the pessary

- **A gel**, which may be given in several doses. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says usually every 6 hours; the Royal Women's Hospital says one, two or three doses, every six to eight hours.
- **A pessary on a tape**, like a tampon, which slowly releases the hormone over 12 to 24 hours.

An oral prostaglandin tablet has also recently been approved and may be used in some situations.

## What happens

After it's put in, you'll be asked to lie down: on your left side for 30 to 40 minutes (Pregnancy, Birth and Baby), or to rest for at least 30 minutes (the Royal Women's Hospital). The baby is monitored for 20 minutes, then every 4 hours, or more often once contractions begin. After 12 to 18 hours you're checked to see whether the cervix is soft and open enough for your waters to be broken. You need to stay in hospital until the baby is born.

When it works, the next steps vary: some people need their waters broken, some need an oxytocin drip, and for some the waters break by themselves. Occasionally more than one dose is needed. If the cervix doesn't open enough, your doctor talks with you about the options, which may include going home until a later date, another method, or a caesarean.

## Who it doesn't suit

Prostaglandins aren't suitable for everyone, for example if you've had a previous caesarean or a reaction to prostaglandins before. A balloon catheter may be recommended instead.

## The risks

- a sore vagina; the Royal Women's Hospital says there's no evidence the labour will be more painful than one that started naturally
- nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea (rare, says the Royal Women's Hospital), low blood pressure or a fast heart rate
- fetal distress
- **contractions too strong or too frequent**, which can stress you and the baby and change the pattern of the baby's heartbeat. You'll be asked to lie on your left side, may be given medicine to relax the uterus, and a pessary may be removed.

## When to get help

Once your induction has begun, tell your doctor or midwife straightaway if you start bleeding or feel the baby moving less.
