An epidural is your choice. It can take away the pain of contractions, can last for hours, and you stay awake and aware, able to push your baby out.

## What happens

An anaesthetist gives it. You'll usually have a drip put in your arm first. You sit and curl forward (or lie on your side) and keep very still while a local anaesthetic numbs your lower back; some people say it feels like a bee sting. The anaesthetist places a needle between the bones of your spine, between contractions, and leaves a very thin soft tube in your back. Local anaesthetic and an opioid go in through the tube and can be topped up; sometimes you control the top-ups with a machine. The tube comes out after the birth.

It usually takes 25 to 40 minutes to set up and take full effect.

## What it means for the rest of labour

- **A drip,** because your blood pressure can fall, and closer blood pressure checks.
- **Continuous monitoring** of the baby with a CTG.
- **A catheter** in your bladder, because you won't feel when you need to wee.
- **Staying in bed.** You can move your legs but may not be able to stand. A few large hospitals offer a walking epidural.
- **Pushing** may be harder, and the second stage may be longer.

On assisted birth (forceps or vacuum), the sources differ. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says an epidural may increase your chance of one. RANZCOG says modern epidurals may slightly lengthen labour but don't change the chance of an assisted birth or a caesarean. Both agree an epidural doesn't make a caesarean more likely.

## Side effects and complications

You may shake or feel itchy, the relief may be patchy or incomplete, and your back may feel bruised for a couple of days. Rarely, some people get a bad headache within 24 to 48 hours. There's a small chance of a skin infection, and very rarely, permanent nerve damage or paralysis. Epidural medicines don't harm your baby.

## The good side

In a long labour it can let you sleep and recover your strength. If you need an emergency caesarean, it can be made stronger so you can stay awake for the birth.

An epidural may be recommended if you're having twins, have high blood pressure, have a higher chance of needing a caesarean, or have a prolonged labour.

## When you can have one

At almost any stage of labour, if there's no medical reason against it (such as a bleeding disorder, an infection or past back surgery). Epidurals are available at most hospitals, but not in birth centres or at home births.

Questions worth asking your midwife or doctor: whether your hospital offers it, whether there are extra costs, what else is available, and the risks for you and your birth.
