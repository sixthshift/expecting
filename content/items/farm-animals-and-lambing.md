Stay away from animals that are giving birth, have just given birth or have miscarried. That means not helping ewes to lamb, cows to calve or goats to kid, and not touching newborn lambs, calves or kids, the afterbirth, or bedding and clothes soiled by it (Darling Downs Health). The same goes if you might be pregnant. Q fever infection in pregnancy can lead to miscarriage, stillbirth, premature birth and low birth weight, and being pregnant gives you a higher chance of the infection becoming long-lasting (healthdirect). It often causes no symptoms in pregnancy, so you may not know you've had it.

## Why

Q fever spreads from animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, dogs, cats and kangaroos, which often look healthy. It's most often caught by breathing in dust particles from dried urine, faeces, placenta or birth fluids. NSW Health says breathing in the bacteria while birthing, slaughtering or butchering infected animals (especially cattle, sheep or goats) carries a very high risk of infection. You can also catch it by handling animals, wool, hides, straw or clothing that has touched them. Toxoplasmosis can also be found in sheep and kangaroos.

The Q fever vaccine is not routinely recommended in pregnancy: it isn't thought harmful, but its safety in pregnancy hasn't been established. It's recommended for people in high-risk jobs, such as farmers, shearers, abattoir and vet workers.

## What to do

- **Wash your hands** after touching animals: scrub with soap and water, and keep your nails short. Hand sanitiser isn't enough here.
- **Leave the farm clothes to someone else.** Don't handle or wash clothes and boots that have touched birthing animals; once washed on a hot cycle, they're safe.
- **Don't move, handle or mow over manure**, fresh or old: it raises dust.
- **If your partner helps with lambing or calving**, they should wear protective clothing and wash well before coming near you.
- **If you work with animals**, wear a fitted P2/N95 mask and gloves, cover wounds, and take off work clothes before going home.
- **Unpasteurised milk:** healthdirect says drinking it may also increase your chance of developing Q fever.
- **Follow your employer's** health and safety advice.
- **See your doctor as soon as possible** if you get a fever or flu-like symptoms, or are worried you've been exposed on a farm, and tell them about the contact. Symptoms usually start 2 to 3 weeks after exposure, but can take from 4 days to 6 weeks.
