Oxytocin is the hormone your body makes in labour to cause contractions. A synthetic version, Syntocinon, is given through a drip in your arm to stimulate contractions.

## When it's used

You're only given oxytocin once your cervix is ripened and your waters have broken. It's often the next step when the waters have been broken but contractions haven't started.

## What happens

The drip is slowly increased until you're having strong, regular contractions. Then the rate is adjusted so they keep coming regularly until the baby is born, which can take several hours. RANZCOG says the infusion continues until after the baby is born.

Once it's started, the baby's heart rate is monitored throughout labour with a CTG machine. Being attached to the drip and the monitor makes it hard to move around: the Royal Women's Hospital says you can stand up or sit down, but you can't have a bath or move from room to room.

## What it can feel like

In an induced labour, contractions can become stronger quickly, where in a natural labour they usually build up slowly, and an induced labour can be more intense and painful. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says there's usually no restriction on the type of pain relief you can have.

## When contractions come too often

Sometimes oxytocin makes the uterus contract too often, without enough time between contractions, which can affect the baby's heart rate. Then:

- you may be asked to lie on your left side
- the drip is slowed, to reduce the number and strength of contractions
- you may be given another medicine to stop the contractions or cancel the oxytocin's effect

Oxytocin can also cause nausea and vomiting.

## When to get help

Once your induction has begun, tell your doctor or midwife straightaway if you start bleeding or feel the baby moving less.
