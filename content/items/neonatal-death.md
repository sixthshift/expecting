A neonatal death, or newborn death, is when a baby dies within the first 4 weeks (28 days) after birth; most happen in the first week. It's different from a stillbirth, when a baby dies between 20 weeks of pregnancy and birth. It's not common, about 700 babies a year in Australia, and it can be devastating for the whole family. However and whenever your baby died, your baby matters, your loss matters, and **your baby's death was not your fault**.

## Why it happens

It's not always known. The risk is higher for babies born prematurely, with a low birthweight or with a congenital condition, and with complications in pregnancy or labour. Some parents ask for an autopsy; it can't be done without your consent unless the death is referred to a coroner, and you can decide how detailed it is. Sometimes no cause is found, even then.

## Time with your baby

This time is precious; take as long as you like. You can hold and cuddle your baby, take lots of photos, keep a lock of hair, and make handprints or footprints. You can ask to take your baby home, or visit them at the funeral home. Do whatever feels right for you. Miracle Babies gives bereaved families a memory box.

## What has to be done

- **Registration.** By law you must register both the birth and the death with Births, Deaths and Marriages in your state or territory.
- **The funeral.** Burial, cremation or a memorial, when you're ready.

A hospital social worker or maternal child health nurse can guide you through these, and family and friends can help. Some parents can get a Family Tax Benefit bereavement payment for up to 14 weeks; Services Australia has the details.

## Your body

If you gave birth, your milk will still come in, which can hurt physically and emotionally. A midwife, child health nurse or lactation consultant can help, and you may choose to donate expressed milk to a milk bank. **See your doctor** if your breasts become painful, swollen and warm, or you develop a fever.

## Grief

Shock, devastation, loneliness, guilt, anxiety and disbelief are common, and grief can bring headaches, tummy aches, insomnia and loss of appetite. It comes in waves and returns around anniversaries and due dates. You and your partner may grieve differently, and it doesn't mean either of you loved the baby less. Talk openly and support each other. It's okay to grieve in private and to see people when you're ready.

Gidget suggests asking one trusted person to update family and friends, and accepting help with meals, transport and paperwork. If it feels unmanageable or affects your ability to function or feel safe, seek support.

## Who helps

- Red Nose Grief and Loss: 1300 308 307, 24/7, including groups for non-birthing parents
- Miracle Babies Nurture Line: 1300 622 243, a free 24/7 family support helpline
- Gidget Foundation, for parents' mental health after loss
- Beyond Blue: 1300 22 4636; ForWhen: 1300 24 23 22

If you or someone close to you is in crisis or at immediate risk of harm, call 000. To talk to someone now, call Lifeline on 13 11 14.
