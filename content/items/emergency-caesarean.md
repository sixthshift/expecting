Even when you plan a vaginal birth, things don't always go as expected. An emergency caesarean is one that needs to happen quickly: either your doctor recommends it once you're in labour, or you need one urgently when you're not in labour. Knowing what happens can make it less frightening, for both of you.

## Why it happens

Your doctor may recommend one if:

- there's concern for your health or your baby's health or wellbeing
- your labour isn't progressing normally
- you have severe bleeding
- you have severe pre-eclampsia
- there's a life-threatening emergency for you or your baby

Your doctor will explain why, talk through the risks, and ask you to sign a consent form. If it's very urgent and there's no time, you may be asked to give verbal consent. You can still ask questions.

## How fast

Doctors follow guidelines with four categories of urgency:

- **Category 1:** an urgent threat to your life or the baby's. Usually within 30 minutes.
- **Category 2:** problems for you or the baby that aren't immediately life threatening. Usually within 60 minutes.
- **Category 3:** the baby needs to be born earlier than planned, but there's no immediate risk.
- **Category 4:** at a time that suits you and the team.

If more than one emergency caesarean is needed at once, the most urgent goes first, so you may have to wait a while even after being told it's an emergency.

## In theatre

You're usually awake, with a spinal or epidural anaesthetic: you'll feel tugging but no pain. A screen goes across your chest. You'll be in theatre for about an hour; the operation takes 30 to 40 minutes and the baby is often born in the first 10.

Usually one support person, such as the partner, can be in theatre with you. Sometimes a general anaesthetic is needed; then the partner isn't in the operating room, because you'll be asleep. Most hospitals have a midwife stay with you and the baby in theatre and in recovery.

If the baby is well, you can have skin-to-skin contact while you're still on the operating table. If the baby has trouble breathing or there are other worries, the paediatrician will care for them, and some babies go to special or intensive care.

## Afterwards

You may feel a range of emotions, especially because it was unexpected. Emergency caesareans can happen very quickly and may be traumatic for some parents and support people, partners included. Talk to a midwife about what happened soon after the birth, and to your doctor, midwife or child health nurse about how you feel. Ask friends and family for practical and emotional support.

If you have negative feelings about the birth, seek help as early as possible: a traumatic birth can lead to post-traumatic stress or postnatal depression, and your doctor can guide you to support. If you, your partner or your support person need to talk, you can call PANDA on 1300 726 306, Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636 or Lifeline on 13 11 14.

After one caesarean, you may still be able to have a vaginal birth next time; talk through the options with your doctor.
