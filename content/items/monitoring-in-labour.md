Your baby's heart rate is checked through labour to see how they're coping. Most babies cope well; changes in the heartbeat pattern can show one who isn't. You have a choice about how it's done, and decisions are made with you and with your consent.

## The options

**Intermittent auscultation (listening at intervals).** A hand-held doppler or a Pinard (a small trumpet-shaped stethoscope) is held on your tummy for about a minute, every 15 to 30 minutes in early labour, and every 5 minutes or after every contraction once you're pushing. It's the usual approach when you're well and labour is uncomplicated.

**Continuous CTG.** Two discs on your tummy, held by belts, record the baby's heart rate and your contractions the whole time. It's recommended when something has been found in the pregnancy or labour that might affect how the baby copes. You'll need it with an epidural, and with an oxytocin drip during an induction.

**Admission CTG.** Some hospitals offer a CTG when you first arrive in labour, stopped if the pattern is normal. Studies haven't shown that doing this for everyone without problems reduces risks to the baby, and it may increase the chance of further interventions.

**Fetal scalp electrode.** If a clear reading can't be got through your tummy, a small wire can be placed on the baby's scalp through your vagina during a vaginal examination. Your waters need to be broken, and there must be no infection risks.

## Things to weigh

| | Listening at intervals | Continuous CTG |
|---|---|---|
| Moving around | More freedom; some inconvenience while they listen | You stay connected; ask if wireless CTG is available |
| Where | Any setting, including in water and at home | Hospital only |
| Picking up changes | Less readily | More readily, which may help some babies |
| Interventions | May mean fewer | Routine use hasn't been shown to prevent serious, rare complications; whether its "false alarms" increase caesareans is debated |

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says routine CTG is not recommended in an uncomplicated (low-risk) labour, because it can lead to unplanned interventions.

## If there are concerns

You might be asked to switch from listening to CTG, or to change position, or the contractions may be slowed. A fetal scalp blood sample or scalp stimulation can give more information. If concerns continue, your team may recommend the baby is born sooner, by caesarean or with forceps or a vacuum.

## When to decide

Your midwife or doctor will talk with you about monitoring before labour, often in the third trimester, and again as labour starts and progresses. If a suggested option doesn't feel right, talk with them about it.
