Your baby's heart is beating by about week 7 of pregnancy. By 25 weeks it beats at about 140 beats a minute, much faster than it will after birth.

## At your appointments

Your midwife or doctor may check your baby's heart rate at your regular antenatal visits, listening through your tummy with a handheld fetal Doppler or a Pinard horn (an ear trumpet). They're checking whether it's too fast, too slow, responding as expected, and showing the right pattern for your baby's age.

The morphology scan, at 18 to 22 weeks, also checks your baby's heart rate and rhythm.

## Later in pregnancy and in labour

A cardiotocograph (CTG) records your baby's heart rate and your contractions continuously, with two sensors held on your tummy by a soft belt. It's sometimes used to check your baby's health during pregnancy.

In labour, if your pregnancy and labour are uncomplicated, your midwife listens at regular intervals with a Doppler or Pinard horn. Routine CTG isn't recommended in labour for low-risk pregnancies, because it can lead to unplanned interventions; it's used when it's clinically needed. If a clear reading is hard to get, a small wire can be placed on your baby's scalp (internal monitoring). Some CTG machines limit how much you can move; you can ask if a wireless one is available. Decisions about monitoring are made with you, and your consent is always needed.

## Home dopplers

Home fetal heart rate monitors are not approved for sale in Australia and are not recommended for use at home. Only trained professionals can accurately assess what they're hearing. At home they can falsely reassure you that your baby is well when they're not, which can delay urgent care and has been linked to serious harm, including baby deaths.

## When to get help

If you're ever concerned about your baby's movement pattern, contact your midwife or doctor immediately. Don't wait until the next day. If you can't reach your maternity care provider, go to your nearest hospital birthing or emergency unit.
