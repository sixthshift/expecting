Yes, it can. Reproductive abuse, also called reproductive violence or reproductive coercion, happens when you are stopped from making your own choices about your reproductive system and health. It can be a form of domestic or family violence, and it can happen in any relationship: a partner or ex-partner, a family member, a carer, or someone you live with.

## What it can look like

Reproductive abuse can include someone:

- forcing or pressuring you to become pregnant
- forcing or pressuring you to have a baby, or to have an abortion
- making you have unprotected sex
- doing things to stop your birth control (the pill, condoms, a diaphragm and others) from working
- hiding your birth control or stopping you from buying it
- stopping you from having emergency contraception, such as the morning after pill, when you need it
- knowingly passing on a sexually transmitted infection to you
- stopping you from getting reproductive healthcare, such as STI screening or cervical screening

None of these people has the right to make you do things you're not comfortable with. What happens to your body should always be your choice.

## It is family violence

Family violence doesn't have to include physical violence or threats. It can be coercive and controlling behaviour, and reproductive abuse is one of the forms it takes. Family violence can start or get worse when you're pregnant, and it's linked to harm to both the pregnant parent and the baby, including miscarriage, premature labour and low birth weight.

It is never your fault, and it is OK to ask for help. The same supports that help with any family violence are there for this.

## Getting help

- **If you're in immediate danger, call 000** for police and ambulance.
- **1800RESPECT** offers confidential counselling, information and referrals, 24/7: call 1800 737 732, text 0458 737 732, or use online chat or video call on its website. It also has resources for making a safety plan.
- **Your doctor or midwife.** Many health services, including maternity hospitals, have social workers and counsellors who can tell you about your rights and help you get protection and support.
- **13YARN** (13 92 76) is crisis support for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. **Lifeline** is 13 11 14.

It may help to bring someone you trust to appointments about your body and your health, as long as it isn't the person pressuring you; you can always ask to be seen on your own. You should never feel pressured to make a decision when you are confused or unsure.

If you work, you're also entitled to 10 days of paid family and domestic violence leave each year, whether you're full-time, part-time or casual.
