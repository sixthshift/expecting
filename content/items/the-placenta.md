The placenta is a temporary organ that grows only in pregnancy. It starts forming when the fertilised egg implants in your uterus, attaches to the uterus lining, and reaches your baby through the umbilical cord.

## What it does

- passes oxygen and nutrients to your baby
- carries waste, such as carbon dioxide, back to your blood for your body to get rid of
- stops some harmful things, such as viruses, reaching your baby
- makes the hormones needed for pregnancy, birth and breastfeeding

It doesn't stop everything. Alcohol, nicotine, some medicines and illicit drugs can cross it and affect your baby.

## Where it sits

The placenta can attach at the top of the uterus, on the sides, at the front (anterior) or at the back (posterior), and it varies from person to person and pregnancy to pregnancy. It sometimes develops low in the uterus, but generally moves higher as the uterus stretches. Its position is checked on ultrasound, early in the first and second trimesters.

Where it sits can change how you feel your baby move. With an anterior placenta, movements tend to be felt around 20 weeks and may not feel as strong. With a posterior placenta, they're often felt earlier and may feel stronger.

## Twins

Fraternal twins each have their own placenta. Identical twins may share one or have one each, and an ultrasound can show which.

## After the birth

You birth the placenta after your baby: the third stage of labour, or the afterbirth. It can be helped along with medicine or come without intervention, and your team will explain the options; it's your choice. Afterwards your midwife or doctor checks it looks complete.

You can take your placenta home. There are no proven health benefits of eating it, and no evidence a lotus birth (leaving the cord attached until the placenta comes away) benefits you or your baby; it may increase complications.

## What can go wrong

- **Placental abruption:** part or all of the placenta comes away from the uterus before birth.
- **Placenta praevia:** the placenta partly or fully covers the cervix.
- **Placental insufficiency:** it doesn't work properly, so your baby may not get enough oxygen and nutrients.
- **Placenta accreta:** it attaches too deeply into the uterus wall, which can lead to severe, life-threatening bleeding during or after birth.
- **Retained placenta:** all or part of it doesn't come out after the birth.

## When to get help

**Call 000** for an ambulance, or go to your nearest emergency department, if you're pregnant and have severe bleeding or significant pain.

**Speak with your doctor or midwife immediately** if you have severe abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding without pain, regular painful contractions before 37 weeks, or any injury to your abdomen, such as from a fall or car accident.

**For bleeding in late pregnancy**, contact your doctor or midwife as soon as possible. Vaginal bleeding in late pregnancy is often due to a placenta problem, Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says. The Royal Women's Hospital says any bleeding in later pregnancy most likely points to a placenta problem and needs medical attention. If you have a low-lying placenta (placenta praevia) and bleed, the blood is yours, not the baby's.
