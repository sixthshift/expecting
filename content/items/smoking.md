Quit if you can. There is no safe amount of smoking in pregnancy. Quitting at any stage of pregnancy is better than carrying on, and quitting completely is the aim, not just cutting down.

## Why

When you smoke, nicotine and other harmful chemicals pass through the placenta to your baby. Smoking in pregnancy raises the risk of:

- miscarriage and stillbirth
- the baby being born early, or small: babies of people who smoke are about 175 to 200 grams lighter
- problems with the placenta, including placental abruption and placenta praevia
- cleft lip and palate, and damage to the baby's heart and lungs

Smoking in pregnancy also raises the baby's risk, once born, of asthma and other lung problems, ear infections and hearing problems, and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).

No other kind is safer. There's no evidence that low-tar, roll-your-own or "natural" tobacco cigarettes are safer than regular cigarettes, and switching to cannabis isn't recommended either. The national pregnancy care guidelines count shisha, chewing tobacco and other tobacco products too.

Cutting down is one way to quit: some people stop all at once, others smoke less over time until they stop completely. But the guidelines stress the benefits of stopping completely, for you and the baby, rather than only cutting down.

## What to do

- **Tell your midwife or doctor.** You'll be asked about smoking and vaping at your first visit and again later in the pregnancy. It's asked so they can help, and the guidelines say the conversation should be respectful and without judgement.
- **Call Quitline on 13 7848** (13 QUIT) to talk to a counsellor or ask for a callback. It can arrange an interpreter. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander counsellors are on the same number (Aboriginal Quitline). There's also the My QuitBuddy app.
- **Ask about nicotine replacement.** The guidelines say you should be offered it as well as support such as Quitline, and that it works better with that support. It's safer than smoking, though not harmless. Its own entry, Nicotine patches, gum and quitting smoking, has the detail.
- **Don't switch to vaping.** Vaping isn't safe in pregnancy and isn't recommended as a way to quit. The prescription quitting medicines varenicline and bupropion aren't recommended in pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- **Make it easier.** Avoid places and situations where you'd usually smoke, and ask family and friends for support. Many people need a few tries to quit for good; every try counts.
- **Make your home and car smoke-free,** for now and after the birth. Red Nose says to keep the baby smoke-free before and after birth, and not to let anyone smoke near the baby, in the house, the car or anywhere else the baby spends time.
- **If you're still smoking when the baby comes,** keep breastfeeding: Red Nose recommends it even if you smoke. Wait a few hours after smoking before a feed, to lower the nicotine and other chemicals the baby gets.

**For the partner:** second-hand smoke harms the baby too, and the guidelines suggest bringing a partner who smokes into the conversation. The partner has an entry of their own on it.
