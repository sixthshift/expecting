Active labour is the second part of the first stage. Contractions get stronger, more painful and more regular, and the cervix opens steadily until it's fully dilated at 10 cm, ready for pushing.

## When it starts

Contractions become regular and painful, every 3 to 4 minutes and lasting around 60 seconds, and the cervix, now fully thinned, starts opening more quickly.

If your pregnancy has been normal and uncomplicated, contact your birth unit when your contractions are regular and about 5 minutes apart, or you can no longer manage at home.

## When you arrive

At the hospital or birth centre, the midwife may:

- feel your tummy to check the baby's position, and ask about the strength and frequency of your contractions
- listen to the baby's heartbeat with a hand-held doppler or a continuous monitor
- ask about your pregnancy, health, medicines and any previous births
- check your blood pressure and pulse, and ask for a urine sample
- with your consent, do a vaginal examination to see how open your cervix is, or a speculum examination to check whether your waters have broken or you're bleeding
- put a cannula in your arm and take bloods, if needed

A folder with your important medical information helps, and so does sharing your birth preferences.

## How long it takes

Your midwife or doctor checks progress by how far the cervix has opened and thinned, how far the baby's head has moved down into your pelvis, and the strength, frequency and length of your contractions. RANZCOG says the active first stage (from 5 cm) usually lasts no more than 18 hours in a first labour and 12 hours in later ones. Its guideline says vaginal examinations shouldn't routinely be planned more often than every four hours in the first stage.

## Coping

Standing and moving about can help with pain and help labour progress. Empty your bladder regularly, so it's easier to change position. You can ask for pain relief at any time, and your midwife will guide you on what suits each stage.

## When it doesn't go to plan

Your team may suggest extra monitoring or medical help if contractions slow or labour isn't progressing, the baby's heart rate drops, there's heavy bleeding or signs of infection, or there's meconium in the waters. They'll explain what's happening and involve you in the decisions.

## When to get help

- **Call 000** if you have heavy bleeding from the vagina, a severe headache or blurred vision, or you think you won't make it to your place of birth before the baby is born.
- **Call your birth unit** if your waters break, you're bleeding from the vagina, or you're worried about the baby's movements. If the baby is moving less, don't wait: see [The baby is moving less](baby-moving-less).
