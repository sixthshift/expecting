Up to 1 in 3 people who give birth see the birth of their baby as traumatic. It's here, before the birth, so you both know it exists, and the partner knows what to look for.

## What it is

Birth trauma can be physical, like an injury, or psychological: a distressing experience in pregnancy, labour or birth that leaves overwhelming emotions, fear and helplessness. It can come from pain, emergency interventions, a long or very fast labour, fear for your baby or yourself, or being separated from your baby. It can also come from how you were treated: not being listened to, feeling forced into decisions, or procedures done without your informed consent. If it felt traumatic to you, it was.

Signs include finding it hard to process what happened, feeling angry, ashamed or sad about it, flashbacks, and feeling your concerns or wishes were dismissed.

## The partner who watched

Birth trauma can affect partners too. You may have found the birth distressing, felt powerless, excluded from decisions or overlooked, or feared that your partner or baby could die. Partners are also vulnerable to depression and anxiety after a birth, and often feel they have to be the strong one. You matter too, and support is there for you.

## When it's PTSD

Feeling dazed, reliving the birth or feeling low is common soon after. Postpartum PTSD, which can affect up to 1 in 10 people 4 to 6 weeks after giving birth, is when it gets worse and keeps affecting your life for 4 weeks or more: flashbacks or nightmares, intrusive memories, hypervigilance, feeling numb and empty, or difficulty bonding with your baby. Untreated birth trauma can turn into PTSD. A doctor or psychologist can diagnose it.

## What helps

- **Talking it through** with your care team can help you understand what happened and why. It may bring relief, though it doesn't work for everyone.
- **Counselling or therapy:** your doctor may recommend counselling, psychological therapy or medication.
- **Peer support:** Birth Trauma Australia offers peer-to-peer support.
- **Practical help:** let family and friends listen, shop and clean.

It is never too late to ask for help; some people seek it months or years after.

## Obstetric violence and your rights

Treatment without your consent, being shouted at, ignored, threatened or refused a support person are forms of obstetric violence. You have the right to give or withdraw consent for any care. If you're unhappy with your care, you can contact the hospital, your state or territory's health complaints body, or AHPRA on 1300 419 495.

## When to get help

If your feelings about the birth are affecting you, see your doctor, midwife or maternal child health nurse. PANDA's helpline is 1300 726 306 and Gidget Foundation is on 1300 851 758.

If you're having thoughts of self-harm or suicide, or are worried about your baby's safety, go to the nearest emergency department or call 000. To talk to someone now, call Lifeline on 13 11 14.
