A balloon catheter is one way to ripen the cervix (soften and open it) at the start of an induction. It's a thin tube with a small balloon on the end, placed in the cervix and filled with water. The pressure of the balloon on the cervix softens and opens it; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says it does this by causing your body to release hormones.

## Who it's for

Prostaglandins aren't suitable for everyone, for example after a previous caesarean or a past reaction to them, so your doctor may recommend a balloon instead. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says a balloon is the preferred way to ripen the cervix if you've had a caesarean before, have given birth more than 5 times, or the baby is measuring small.

You can't have one if your waters have broken, you have bleeding without a clear diagnosis, or the baby's monitoring (such as their heart rate or movements) isn't as expected.

## What happens

The other end of the catheter is taped to your inner thigh, so gentle tension helps the balloon slowly move down. You and the baby are monitored for about 20 minutes or more.

What happens next depends on the hospital:

- The Royal Women's Hospital: you stay in hospital but can move around normally, the midwives check you and the baby's heart from time to time, and the catheter is left in for up to 12 hours.
- Pregnancy, Birth and Baby: you may be able to go home after monitoring and come back 12 hours later, depending on your local health service.
- RANZCOG: it stays in for several hours, until it falls out (a sign your cervix has opened) or you're examined again.

If your waters break or contractions get too strong, the balloon is removed. Afterwards you're reassessed. You might need another balloon or another method, your waters broken, or an oxytocin drip to start contractions.

## The risks

- discomfort after it's put in, and the examination itself can be uncomfortable
- difficulty passing urine
- bleeding, or damage to the cervix
- placental abruption (the placenta coming away from the wall of the uterus)
- a tear (rupture) of the uterus

## When to get help

Once your induction has begun, tell your doctor or midwife straightaway if you start bleeding or feel the baby moving less.
