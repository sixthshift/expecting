Most people with epilepsy have healthy babies. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby lists epilepsy among the pre-existing medical conditions that may make a pregnancy higher risk. Epilepsy Action Australia says that before trying to conceive you should talk to your neurologist, GP and/or an obstetrician, and that they will want to monitor you closely throughout.

## If you're pregnant and on medicine

Epilepsy Action Australia's most important advice if you find yourself unexpectedly pregnant while taking antiseizure medicine: don't panic, and **do not stop taking your medicines**. Contact your doctor to discuss your options. For most people it's best to continue treatment in pregnancy, and doctors are advised to avoid withdrawing or changing antiseizure medicines once you're already pregnant.

## What the team will talk about

- **Which medicine and dose.** Your neurologist will ideally aim to use one medication at the lowest possible dose to achieve the best seizure control for you. People taking antiseizure medicine have a slightly higher chance of a baby with a birth defect (4 to 6%, against 2 to 3% in the general population), depending on the medicine, how many and the dose.
- **Valproate.** Babies exposed to sodium valproate in pregnancy, especially at higher doses, have a higher rate of birth defects and of developmental problems. Epilepsy Action Australia says doctors are advised to avoid sodium valproate if an equally effective antiseizure medication is available, and that it isn't prescribed for girls or women of childbearing age unless other antiseizure medications are not effective or cause intolerable side effects. The TGA says it is well known that valproate can cause malformations and neurodevelopmental disorders in babies born to women taking it before and during pregnancy, and that women and people who can give birth to children are advised to avoid taking it at those times. If you're on it and unexpectedly pregnant, Epilepsy Action Australia's advice is the same as for any antiseizure medicine: do not stop taking your medications; contact your doctor to discuss your options.
- **Dose changes.** Pregnancy can change how your body absorbs and clears some medicines, so some medicines may need to be monitored and increased whether or not you're having seizures, and your blood levels may be monitored throughout your pregnancy. If the dose is changed for pregnancy, it is most likely to be returned to pre-pregnancy levels shortly after delivery.
- **Folate.** A recommended folate supplement, ideally from at least 3 months before pregnancy and through the first trimester.
- **The birth.** Most pregnant people with epilepsy deliver without complications, and the choice of pain relief is no different from other pregnant people. If you've had frequent seizures during pregnancy, especially in the third trimester, there is an increased risk of a seizure during delivery; you'll need more frequent appointments with both your obstetrician and neurologist, and the safest delivery method will be discussed.

## Seizures in pregnancy

For most, seizures stay the same; for some they ease, and for others, especially if sleep or medicine levels are affected, they increase. A tonic-clonic seizure can briefly reduce the baby's oxygen and heart rate, and a severe fall or serious injury can injure the baby. If you have a seizure while pregnant, report it to your neurologist; they may need to check your medication levels or adjust your medication.

## More help

Epilepsy Action Australia's National Epilepsy Line is 1300 37 45 37. The Australian Pregnancy Register for Women on Antiseizure Medication is a national study you can join while pregnant or up to 12 months after the birth.
