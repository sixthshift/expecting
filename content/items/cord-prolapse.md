## When to get help

**If you're pregnant, your waters have broken and you feel the umbilical cord in your vagina, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.** While you wait, get onto your hands and knees, then lower your head and chest so your bottom is in the air, higher than your head. This takes pressure off the cord. Stay like this until help arrives.

If the baby isn't head down and your waters break, **call triple zero (000)** and ask for an ambulance; if labour starts, go to hospital straight away.

## What it is

A cord prolapse is when the umbilical cord moves in front of the baby, so it comes through the cervix before the baby, or with them. It can only happen once your waters have broken, usually during labour. It's rare, about 1 in 600 births, but it's an emergency: the cord can be squashed between the baby and the birth canal, which cuts the baby's oxygen.

## The signs

- feeling the cord in your vagina, or seeing it coming out (it's shiny and bluish-white)
- your midwife or doctor seeing it during an examination
- a change in the baby's movements, or their heart rate slowing
- meconium (the baby's poo) in the waters

Sometimes there are no signs.

## Who it's more likely for

It's more likely if you've had one or more babies before, if there's too much amniotic fluid, if you've had a baby who weighed under 2.5 kg at birth, or if you have a different pelvic structure. It's also more likely if the baby isn't head down, has birth differences, has a very long cord, is the second twin or is premature. If you have a higher chance, your team checks for it at every vaginal examination. Cord prolapse can't be prevented, but if your baby is lying on its side or keeps changing position, your midwife or doctor may recommend staying in hospital from 37 weeks, so help is at hand when your waters break or you go into labour. If the cord is felt before your waters have broken, your doctor will not break your waters if possible.

## What the team does

An emergency caesarean is usually recommended, sometimes under a general anaesthetic so the baby is born more quickly. Meanwhile your midwife or doctor gently lifts the baby's head off the cord, and may fill your bladder with water through a catheter to help lift it. You may be given medicine to stop contractions. If you're close to giving birth, a vaginal or assisted birth may be possible.

## Afterwards

Most babies have no complications, though a baby who didn't get enough oxygen can have brain damage; it's very rare for a baby to die from a cord prolapse. It can be frightening for you and your partner, so talk it over with your midwife or doctor, or a counsellor. It's unlikely to happen again in a later pregnancy.
