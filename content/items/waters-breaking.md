Your waters breaking means the amniotic sac around your baby has broken and the fluid is leaking out of your vagina. It's a normal, healthy part of late pregnancy or early labour, and it means your baby is getting ready to be born. For most people it happens during labour; for some it happens before labour starts.

## What it's like

The fluid may come as a gush or a trickle. You may feel as if you've wet yourself. It usually isn't painful, and you may not feel anything at all.

Check the colour. It's usually clear, but it can be yellow, straw-coloured or pink.

## What to do

Put on a sanitary pad and contact your midwife, doctor or birth centre. They'll tell you when to stop what you're doing and go to the place you plan to give birth. If you're out in public, stay calm and ask for help if you need it.

If your midwife or doctor thinks your waters have broken, they'll examine you with a speculum. If they're still not sure, they can test the vaginal fluid for amniotic proteins.

## If labour hasn't started

When waters break before labour, it's called pre-labour rupture of membranes (PROM). From 37 weeks, most people go into labour on their own within 24 to 96 hours, and most give birth within 24 to 48 hours of their waters breaking. Your midwife or doctor will talk through your options: waiting for labour to start on its own, or an induction to reduce the risk of infection. Your choices and preferences will be respected.

If you've tested positive for group B strep (GBS), your team will recommend antibiotics during labour, which lowers the chance of passing it to your baby, and may recommend an induction as soon as possible.

If your waters break 18 hours or more before the birth, your baby will need their vital signs checked for their first 24 hours.

## When to get help

**If your waters break and you've been told the baby isn't head-first, call triple zero (000)** and ask for an ambulance: there's a higher risk of cord prolapse.

**If your waters break before 37 weeks,** contact your midwife or doctor and go to the hospital. There's a higher chance of early birth or infection.

**If the fluid is green, brown or blood-coloured,** call your doctor, midwife or birthing unit immediately for advice, or go to hospital. Green or brown may mean your baby has passed meconium and may be distressed; blood-coloured could be a sign of placental abruption, which is very rare.

**Call your midwife, doctor or hospital immediately** if your waters break and:

- they smell bad
- you're bleeding from your vagina
- you have a temperature of 37.5°C or above
- you feel unwell or have flu-like symptoms
- you notice a change in your baby's movements
- you're having regular painful contractions or pain in your belly
