If you took something before you found out you're pregnant, the answer depends on the medicine and when you took it, so tell your doctor, pharmacist or midwife what you took.

## Why

Every pregnancy starts with a 3 to 5% chance of a birth defect, called the background risk, and medicines account for only a small part of it.

How a medicine affects the baby depends on when in the pregnancy it's taken. In very early pregnancy, a harmful exposure most likely causes an "all or nothing" response: either a miscarriage, or no significant effect on the pregnancy. In the first trimester, when the baby's major structures form, harmful exposures are most likely to cause birth defects. Later, they're more likely to cause growth problems, preterm birth or withdrawal symptoms in the newborn.

**Anti-inflammatories** such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and aspirin are no longer recommended in pregnancy except under medical supervision. But MotherSafe says that people who have accidentally used one can generally be reassured, though there are various reasons to avoid them at different stages of pregnancy.

## What to do

- **Don't stop or change a regular medicine on your own.** Before starting, continuing, changing or stopping any medicine in pregnancy, talk to a doctor, pharmacist or midwife.
- **For specific medicines,** call MotherSafe, the NSW medicines in pregnancy service: 9382 6539 (Sydney metro) or 1800 647 848 (non-metro), Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm.
