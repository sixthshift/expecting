Many people in their mid-30s and older go on to have healthy pregnancies. Age is one factor among many. What it does change is the odds of some complications, and so the care you're offered.

## What changes with age

As you get older, the chance of pregnancy complications increases. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby lists:

- pre-eclampsia
- gestational diabetes
- miscarriage: being older is one of the things that makes it more likely
- chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome

## Why you may hear "higher risk"

Being over 35 is on Pregnancy, Birth and Baby's list of things that can make a pregnancy higher risk. That's a statement about chances, not a prediction. In practice your midwife or doctor watches the pregnancy more closely, which may mean more blood tests, scans or appointments.

## What to do

Good antenatal care matters. It's recommended to have your first antenatal visit before 10 weeks. Talk to your doctor or midwife about:

- prenatal screening and testing for chromosomal conditions
- genetic counselling, if you'd like help understanding the options or a result

## If you're still trying

Fertility drops more after the age of 35, and getting pregnant may become more difficult for some. See your doctor if you're 35 or older and have been trying for 6 months or more, or sooner if you have endometriosis or another condition that may affect fertility. Trying can be emotionally hard, especially when it takes longer than expected; counselling and peer support are there if you want them.
