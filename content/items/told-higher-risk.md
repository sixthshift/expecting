A high-risk pregnancy means you or your baby has a greater chance of problems during the pregnancy or birth. It's about chances: the specific risks depend on your situation and are different in every case.

## What can put you there

**Your health and background**, such as:

- being under 17 or over 35
- pre-existing conditions such as epilepsy, diabetes, asthma, kidney disease, heart disease, autoimmune disease, or any condition you have ongoing care for
- high blood pressure or a high risk of pre-eclampsia
- living with obesity
- mental health conditions
- drinking alcohol, smoking or using drugs
- limited social support, isolation or past experiences of violence

**Your baby**, such as twins or more, a baby measuring small, or signs you may give birth early.

**A previous pregnancy** with a loss, a preterm birth, diabetes in pregnancy or pre-eclampsia.

## What changes

Your midwife or doctor watches the pregnancy more closely and may be more involved. That can mean more blood tests, more ultrasounds, or appointments with obstetricians who specialise in high-risk pregnancies, such as maternal-fetal medicine specialists. You may need to start a medicine. With high blood pressure, you'll go to hospital or a health centre for blood tests, blood pressure checks and monitoring of your baby's heart rate.

## The birth

The type and timing of birth depend on your needs. You may need to give birth in a hospital or birth unit with the right staff and equipment for your or your baby's condition. Your team may recommend an induction if continuing the pregnancy adds risk, or a caesarean, planned or in an emergency. They should explain why, and the risks and benefits of having or not having it, so you can give informed consent.

## What to do

Have your first antenatal visit before 10 weeks if you can: early care may be linked to better health for you and your baby. Bring your questions to each appointment; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby's maternal child health nurses are on 1800 882 436, 7am to midnight (AET), every day.
