The village has changed. Many parents live away from their families and don't have a close network of friends, so there's less help when it's most needed. New parents often leave work, social groups and sport behind and spend long stretches home alone with the baby, and that isolation, on top of everything else changing, can make a mental health problem more likely. Gidget's advice is to build a support network as part of planning for the baby. Asking for help doesn't mean you're not coping or not a good parent.

## Make the list before the baby comes

Write a list of people your partner or family can call on. Gidget suggests checking that each person:

- is actually available
- can listen without judging or dismissing

And that the list includes:

- outside support services
- a backup plan for when your usual people can't come
- support you can reach if you live rural or remote

It's worth thinking about what makes asking for help hard for you, too.

## Who can be in it

- friends nearby who can drop in during the day
- friends or family for phone or video calls
- other new or expectant parents you already know
- your midwife, maternal health nurse, lactation consultant or GP
- a new parents' group: maternal health centres usually run them after the birth
- online groups, which can suit you if leaving home is hard or you live far out

Social media can connect you too, but use it with caution: many parents find the information unreliable and the picture of parenting unrealistic.

## What to ask for

People often want to help but don't know how. Gidget's list for supporters gives you words for it: a meal that reheats easily, some shopping, cleaning, a cake, taking an older child to the park, checking before visiting, a message that doesn't need a reply, and just listening. Emotional and practical help both count.

## If you're on your own

PBB says other single parents may be your best source of support. Local groups, your council and swap arrangements with other parents (taking turns minding the children) can all help.

## When it's more than tired

Perinatal depression and anxiety affect 1 in 5 mums and 1 in 10 partners or dads. Gidget tells supporters to take it seriously if someone seems low, overwhelmed, quick to anger or distant from the baby, and to help them get support, such as an appointment with their GP. Words like "my family would be better off without me" need to be taken seriously. Lifeline gives 24/7 crisis support on 13 11 14; call 000 if anyone is in immediate danger.
