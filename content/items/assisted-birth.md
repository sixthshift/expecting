Sometimes a baby needs a hand to be born through the vagina, with a vacuum cup (ventouse) or forceps. In Australia and New Zealand almost 1 baby in 10 is born this way, and it's most common in a first birth. It's worth talking about with your midwife or doctor, and your birth partner, before labour, because birth doesn't always go to plan.

## Why it's needed

- the baby isn't moving down the birth canal as expected
- there are concerns for the baby's wellbeing and the birth needs to happen quickly
- the baby is in a position that makes it hard to be born without help
- you're exhausted and can't push effectively, or you've been advised not to push

## How it's done

It's done only with your informed consent. Your doctor examines you to check your cervix is fully open and where the baby's head is, makes sure you have enough pain relief (a local anaesthetic, or an epidural or spinal), and empties your bladder with a catheter. Sometimes it's done in an operating theatre, usually if a caesarean might still be needed.

- **Vacuum:** a soft cup on the baby's head, held by suction. As you push with a contraction, the doctor gently pulls. It's used more often than forceps.
- **Forceps:** two smooth, spoon-shaped blades around the baby's head, again pulling as you push. With forceps you'll usually need an episiotomy (a cut to make the vaginal opening bigger); with a vacuum you may.

More than one pull may be needed. If the baby isn't born after 3 pulls with the cup, the doctor may try forceps. In many cases the baby is born straight onto your chest.

## The risks

Most assisted births happen without complications, and it's almost always done because the risk of not getting the baby out is greater.

**For you:** bruising or injury to the skin around the vagina, a perineal tear, and injury to the pelvic floor, which can affect bladder or bowel function.

**For the baby:** a mark or bruise on the head or cheeks that soon fades, a collection of blood under the scalp (more common with the vacuum), jaundice, and rarely weakness in the face from a nerve injury that usually heals quickly. Severe injury to the head or spine is very rare.

## When it becomes a caesarean

If the attempt doesn't work, or it becomes too dangerous to keep going, the doctor stops and you have an emergency caesarean. When the baby's head is very deep in the birth canal, a caesarean isn't an easy option either. You can also choose not to have an assisted birth and keep pushing, or have a caesarean instead.

Questions you can ask in the moment: why do you think I need it, what does it involve, what are the risks of having it and of not having it, and are there other options?

## Afterwards

Recovery takes a few weeks. Ask to talk through what happened before you leave hospital. See your doctor if you have pain in the vagina, trouble controlling your bladder or bowels, or pain during sex that isn't getting better.
