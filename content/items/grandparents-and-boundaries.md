Family and friends are often excited, and Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says people can offer help in all sorts of ways. It also says you may sometimes need to put limits on outside help, for example if it feels as if they're taking over, and that you might get a lot of advice, and even some criticism. The important thing is to decide what's right for you.

## Unwanted advice

The Gidget Foundation says a baby's arrival often brings an influx of advice from family, peers and even strangers. Most comes from people who want to feel useful, but unsolicited advice, information overload and inaccurate information can undermine new parents' confidence.

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby suggests explaining gently that some decisions are only for you and your partner to make. The Gidget Foundation suggests other options too:

- deflect it with humour, or just say "thanks"
- give a nod, then disengage, or divert by asking the person to help
- ask a friend or partner to be an advocate
- if you need to be firmer, practise a couple of sentences, such as "Thank you for trying to help. I really appreciate that you care about me and the baby. We are going to take our time with trying a few different things and we'll work it out."

## Hard conversations

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says to pick a time when you're both calm, explain your point of view and listen with an open mind. It's OK to suggest stopping if either of you feels angry, emotional or overwhelmed, and to try again when calmer.

## Visitors after the birth

Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says a lot of people may want to meet your baby, and that's great, but it can be tiring. It's up to you how many visitors you have, and when, and if you're exhausted you can avoid visitors for a while.

## For grandparents and friends

The Gidget Foundation says to ask "what can I do for you?" and listen, check before visiting and respect the parents' need for space, offer practical help (a meal, taking a toddler to the park, some shopping or cleaning), stay in touch without expecting a quick reply, and generally avoid giving parenting advice. It notes the "village" has changed, with many families living far from their families of origin, so support is harder to find when it's most needed.
