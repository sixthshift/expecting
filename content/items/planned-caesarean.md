A planned (elective) caesarean is a birth through a cut in your tummy and uterus, booked before labour starts. Around 2 in every 5 babies in Australia are born by caesarean. It's a birth like any other, and it's a major operation, so your doctor will explain the risks and benefits and you'll sign a consent form. In Australia you have the right to make the decision together with your doctor.

## The options

**A caesarean your doctor recommends.** Reasons include:

- you've had a caesarean before
- the baby is breech (bottom or feet first) or lying sideways, and can't be turned
- twins, where the first twin is breech
- placenta praevia, where the placenta covers the opening of the uterus
- other medical problems for you or the baby

**A caesarean you ask for.** You may prefer a caesarean for non-medical reasons. Your doctor or midwife will explain the benefits and risks of every option so you can make an informed choice. RANZCOG notes that some risks may not show until later pregnancies.

**A planned vaginal birth**, where there's no medical reason for a caesarean.

## Things to weigh

- **For you.** Major complications are rare, but they include blood loss, wound infection, blood clots in the legs or lungs, damage to nearby organs such as the bladder, anaesthetic side effects, and a slower recovery.
- **For the baby.** The most common problem is temporary breathing difficulty. Babies born before 39 weeks by planned caesarean have more breathing problems, which is why it's usually booked at 39 weeks or later. If there are problems in the pregnancy, or more than one baby, it may be earlier.
- **Future pregnancies.** After one caesarean, future pregnancies are treated as higher risk, and the risk of rare but serious complications (such as the placenta growing low, near the scar, or not coming away) rises with each caesarean. RANZCOG suggests thinking about the size of the family you want.
- **Recovery.** You'll usually stay in hospital 3 to 5 days. Most people feel well by about six weeks. Don't drive until you've fully recovered and the wound has healed, which may take up to 6 weeks; your obstetrician can advise. Plan for plenty of help at home.

## What to ask

- **Who can be there?** Usually one support person, such as the partner, can be with you in theatre. Not if you need a general anaesthetic, because you'll be asleep.
- **Which anaesthetic?** Most people have a spinal or epidural and are awake, feeling tugging but no pain. Your doctor will talk through the options.
- **Skin to skin?** If the baby is well, you or your support person can hold the baby skin to skin while you're still on the operating table.
- **Photos?** Ask whether your support person can take photos or video.

## When to decide

With your doctor, during the pregnancy, so the date can be booked, usually at 39 weeks or later.

If labour starts before your booked date, call your hospital's maternity unit immediately. Many people do go into labour first, and it then becomes an emergency rather than a planned caesarean.
