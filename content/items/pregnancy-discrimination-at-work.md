Under the Fair Work Act, an employer can't take adverse action against you, or against someone applying for a job, because of pregnancy, breastfeeding, or family or carer's responsibilities. The protection applies from the job application, before you start, and at any time while you work there.

## What counts as adverse action

Doing, threatening or organising any of these:

- firing you
- injuring you in your employment, for example not giving you your legal entitlements such as pay or leave
- changing your job to your disadvantage
- treating you differently from other employees
- not hiring you, or offering you worse terms than other employees

The Fair Work Ombudsman's own example: a manager tells all the account managers they can go to a training course, then tells the one who has just announced her pregnancy that she can't go because she's pregnant. That's discrimination.

Adverse action isn't always discrimination. It can be lawful when it has nothing to do with the pregnancy, such as genuine performance problems, or relates to the necessary requirements of the job.

## Using your rights is protected too

Parental leave and the rest of your entitlements under the Fair Work Act are workplace rights. It's unlawful to take adverse action against you because you have, use or plan to use one. It's also unlawful to misrepresent them: telling a part-time employee that only full-timers get parental leave, knowing that isn't true, is against the law.

After unpaid parental leave you're entitled to come back to the job you had before, even if someone else has been doing it. If you moved to a safe job or cut your hours because of the pregnancy, you return to the job you had before that. You also can't be discriminated against for breastfeeding at work.

Separately, under the Sex Discrimination Act, employers have a positive duty to eliminate, as far as possible, discrimination on the ground of sex and sex-based harassment at work.

## Where to go

- **The Fair Work Ombudsman,** on 13 13 94, to talk it through. It can investigate most types of unlawful workplace discrimination, or refer you to a body better placed to help. A union or employee association may also help.
- **The Fair Work Commission,** if you haven't been dismissed: you can apply to it to deal with a general protections dispute.
- **The Fair Work Commission, within 21 days, if you've been dismissed.** The 21 days start the day after the dismissal. The Commission tries to resolve it with you and the employer; if that fails, it issues a certificate and you have 14 days to go to court, or both sides can agree to arbitration.
- **The Australian Human Rights Commission** is another body you can complain to. Making a complaint or enquiry like that is itself a protected workplace right.
