The umbilical cord is attached to the placenta and to your baby. It gives your baby the nutrients and oxygen they need to grow, and takes waste products away. After the birth the cord is clamped and cut, leaving a stump, which eventually falls off and heals to form the umbilicus (belly button).

## A single umbilical artery

Safer Care Victoria's clinical guidance says the cord normally has two arteries, and in a single umbilical artery (sometimes called a two-vessel cord) there is only one. It may be associated with other birth defects. It can be seen on a scan, though your baby's position can make it harder to see. The guidance says that when it's found before birth, management includes a thorough scan and a scan of the baby's heart, and may include chromosome testing. If it's the only finding, it says there should be close follow-up of the baby's growth with repeat ultrasounds as clinically indicated. After the birth, babies with a single artery and no other problems should have a routine postnatal examination. Note this is clinical guidance for health professionals, first published in December 2017 with a review due by December 2020, so it is dated.

## Cord blood

Cord blood is the blood left in the cord and placenta after the birth. It's normally thrown away, but it can be collected, which takes about 3 minutes after the cord is cut and doesn't hurt you or your baby. In Australia you can donate to an AusCord public cord blood bank, which is free, or store it in a private bank, which charges fees. You need to arrange it before the birth, and for donation you need to give birth in a participating hospital. Deciding to bank cord blood makes no difference to your birth plan.

## When to get help

A cord prolapse is rare (about 1 in 600 births) but an emergency, because it can reduce your baby's oxygen. It's when the cord moves in front of the baby after your waters have broken. If your waters have broken and you feel the cord in your vagina, **call 000 and ask for an ambulance**. You may be asked to bring your knees to your chest with your head down while you wait. If your baby is not head down, Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says to go to hospital urgently if your waters break or you think you're going into labour.
