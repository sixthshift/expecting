Once a miscarriage is confirmed, there are three ways to manage it. The treatment depends on your circumstances and the type of miscarriage, so ask your doctor which are open to you. You should discuss the options with your doctor or midwife.

## The options

**Expectant (natural) management: waiting.** You wait for your body to pass the pregnancy tissue on its own. Bleeding and cramping usually start within 7 to 14 days of the pregnancy ending, building to heavy bleeding and stronger cramps, sometimes with large clots. The pain and bleeding should ease within 1 to 2 days, and some bleeding or spotting may go on for about 2 weeks. Your doctor will want to see you within 7 to 10 days for a blood test, and maybe an ultrasound. It usually takes longer than the other options.

**Medical management: medicine.** Misoprostol, alone or with mifepristone, helps the cervix open and the uterus contract so the tissue comes away. Usually you take it at home; sometimes in hospital. For most people the tissue starts to pass about 4 to 6 hours later, with heavier bleeding, clots and strong period-like cramps. Bleeding can take up to 2 weeks to stop. You'll have a follow-up and a blood test a week later; if tissue stays behind, you may need surgery. It is usually not recommended if you've already lost a lot of blood, have an infection or high blood pressure, or are allergic to the medicines.

**Surgical management: a short operation.** A dilatation and curettage (D&C) is done through the vagina, often under general anaesthetic, and takes about 10 minutes. Light bleeding and cramps last 7 to 14 days. The risks are very low: up to 3 in 100 get an infection that needs antibiotics. It is often recommended if you have heavy bleeding or signs of infection, or if the other options haven't worked.

## Things to weigh

PBB names what draws some people to each. Waiting may feel more natural, help with grieving and give a sense of control, though the waiting itself can be emotionally draining. Surgery may suit you if you want to limit pain and bleeding, or don't want to wait for the other options to work. Some people would rather have a planned procedure in hospital than wait at home and possibly need emergency surgery later.

Whichever you choose, plan a follow-up with your doctor 4 to 6 weeks after the miscarriage.

## When to decide

There is no set deadline in the sources. If you're waiting and the tissue hasn't passed after 1 to 2 weeks, speak to your doctor: you might keep waiting if you feel well, or choose treatment. Medical or surgical treatment is sometimes needed to avoid heavy bleeding and prevent infection.

At any point, see your doctor or go to the nearest emergency department if you have pain worse than period pain, are soaking 1 to 2 pads an hour, pass clots the size of a golf ball, have unusual or smelly discharge, a fever of 38°C or more or chills, feel dizzy, lightheaded or faint, or have heavy bleeding for more than 2 weeks. If you have very heavy bleeding, strong pain or feel unwell, call 000 and ask for an ambulance.
