Twins are more likely than single babies to be born early, often before 38 weeks. For an uncomplicated twin pregnancy (monochorionic or dichorionic), the Queensland clinical guideline says to plan birth after 37+0 weeks; it also says to consider all clinical circumstances and consult an expert practitioner about the mode and timing of birth.

You still have choices: where you give birth, who cares for you (including a midwife), and whether the babies are born vaginally or by caesarean. Your doctor or midwife may advise a hospital birth, because complications are more likely with twins. Plan your choices early, and know what might happen if the birth doesn't go to plan: a choice may not be possible if there's a risk to you or a baby.

## Who's in the room

Several staff are commonly involved: a midwife, an obstetrician, 2 paediatricians and 2 extra nurses or midwives, one for each baby.

## A vaginal birth

About 1 in 3 twin births are vaginal. Labour is the same as with one baby, but the babies are closely checked: their heart rates may be monitored, and you may have a drip put in, in case it's needed later.

- Head down (cephalic) is the usual position for the first twin when a vaginal birth goes ahead.
- Your team may recommend an **epidural**, because if there are problems it's easier and quicker to help with the birth when you already have good pain relief.
- You may need an assisted birth with forceps or a vacuum cup.
- After the first baby, the midwife or doctor checks the second baby's position by feeling your tummy, and may do a vaginal examination or bedside ultrasound. If the position is good, your waters may be broken, and the second baby should come soon after, since your cervix is already fully open. If contractions stop, you may be given medicine to strengthen and increase them.

## A caesarean

You can choose a planned caesarean, or it may be recommended as the end nears. It's usually recommended if the first twin is breech, and needed if one twin is lying sideways (transverse) or with placenta praevia. You're about twice as likely to have a caesarean with twins.

Even with a planned vaginal birth, an emergency caesarean may be needed: if a baby becomes distressed, the cord prolapses, your blood pressure is too high, labour is too slow or an assisted birth doesn't work. Very rarely, the first twin is born vaginally and the second by emergency caesarean.

## Afterwards

One or both babies may need time in special care or neonatal intensive care. The hospital will try to keep you all together; if one baby goes home first, ask whether you can bring them when you visit, and whether co-bedding is allowed.
