Colostrum is your first breast milk: a thick, sticky, yellowish liquid, made from about 16 weeks. There's not much of it, but it's very high in protein, including antibodies, and easy for a newborn to digest.

**Who it can help.** Expressing and storing colostrum after 36 weeks can help if your baby is likely to have feeding problems: if you have diabetes (the baby may have trouble keeping their blood sugar up), if the baby may need special care and be separated from you, if there's a congenital condition that could affect feeding, or if you may have a low supply (for example after breast surgery or with polycystic ovarian syndrome). It builds a store for extra feeds instead of formula top-ups. Talk to your doctor or midwife about whether it's a good idea for you.

**Who shouldn't.** Not before 36 weeks: stimulating the breasts may trigger premature labour. Don't express if you've had, are at risk of, or are having premature labour; have a cervical suture or a short cervix; have had bleeding in the pregnancy; have placenta praevia; have been advised not to; or can't store colostrum safely.

**How.** Use your hands only. Wash your hands, then with your thumb above and fingers below, press gently towards the nipple, compress, hold briefly and release, without pinching the nipple. Move around the areola and swap sides after 2 to 3 minutes. Collect it in a sterile syringe or clean container. You can collect twice a day into the same syringe, keeping it in the fridge in between. Don't be discouraged if you only express a little, or nothing at first; colostrum is usually made in small amounts.

**Storing.** Label with the date and time, cap the syringe, and freeze it in a plastic bag. It keeps 2 weeks in a freezer compartment inside a fridge, 3 months in a fridge-freezer with its own door, or 6 to 12 months in a deep freezer.

**Stop** if you feel contractions or notice vaginal bleeding.

**Taking it in.** Add your name and date of birth, and bring it frozen in a cool bag or esky when you go to have your baby.
