A molar pregnancy is a rare type of pregnancy loss. An egg is fertilised but a baby doesn't develop; instead, the cells that normally form the placenta grow into a clump of abnormal cells. It's also called a hydatidiform mole or gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD). It wasn't caused by anything you did or didn't do.

## Complete or partial

- In a **complete** molar pregnancy, no embryo develops at all.
- In a **partial** molar pregnancy, a fetus can develop but is abnormal and cannot survive.

## How it's found

The pregnancy hormone hCG is still made, often at higher-than-normal levels, so you can feel pregnant, with sore breasts and morning sickness that can be severe. Other signs can include vaginal bleeding, a dark discharge, or a uterus that's larger than expected. Some people have no symptoms and find out at a routine ultrasound, or after a miscarriage or termination when the tissue is examined. Your doctor may order an ultrasound and a blood test for your hCG level.

## Treatment

A molar pregnancy needs to be removed to avoid complications. A specialist removes the tissue with a D&C (dilatation and curettage), usually under general anaesthetic, and you can usually go home the same day. The tissue is tested in a laboratory. For most people, that's enough treatment.

Sometimes some molar tissue stays and grows deeper into the uterus and spreads (an invasive mole), which needs more treatment. Rarely, it turns into a cancer called choriocarcinoma, which also needs more treatment.

## Why the follow-up

The follow-up checks that the molar tissue has completely gone:

- **weekly blood tests** for your hCG level until it returns to normal
- then **monthly blood tests for another 6 months**

A molar pregnancy doesn't affect your chances of getting pregnant again, but you'll be advised to wait for your doctor's all-clear before trying. Ask your specialist about the best contraception while you wait.

## The waiting

Losing a pregnancy can be devastating however it happens, and the long follow-up can make it hard to move forward. You may feel sad, shocked and confused, especially if you'd never heard of this before, and anxious about the future. Give yourself time to grieve, and talk with supportive family and friends.
