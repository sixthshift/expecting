This one is for the partner, because the person who is unwell may not be able to see it, and may resist help. Postpartum psychosis (also called postnatal or puerperal psychosis) is a rare but serious mental illness (the Royal Women's says very rare). It can come on very suddenly, within hours of the birth, or up to 12 weeks after. It is different from postnatal depression, which is more common.

## What it looks like

Psychosis means losing a sense of reality. The signs usually come as a mix of:

- confusion about the day, the time or who people are; being disorganised or forgetful
- not being able to sleep, sometimes for days, or feeling like you need less sleep
- mood that swings: very high, irritable or depressed
- agitation, so they can't keep still; speaking quickly, not finishing sentences, making unrealistic plans
- beliefs that aren't true, often paranoid: that hospital staff are spies, that you're an imposter
- hearing, seeing or smelling things that aren't there
- being unable to care for the baby
- thoughts of, or plans for, harming themselves or the baby

## Who is more at risk

It can affect anyone who gives birth, even with no history of mental illness. The risk is higher with a personal or family history of bipolar disorder or postpartum psychosis, severe sleep deprivation, or a physically stressful or complicated birth. If any of that applies, the doctor may plan care through pregnancy and after the birth with a psychiatrist or perinatal mental health team, and may recommend close monitoring after the birth or preventive medicine.

## What to do

The Royal Women's calls it a psychiatric emergency that needs immediate treatment, for the safety of the parent and the baby. PBB says that if you're worried your partner may have it, **arrange for a doctor or mental health professional to see them urgently**, for the safety of both the birth parent and the baby. You can start with their GP or your local hospital emergency department; the Royal Women's also lists the hospital where the baby was born. Delaying treatment can make recovery longer. Your local hospital or community health centre can also put you in touch with a crisis team (sometimes called a CAT team).

PBB adds: **if they're having thoughts of self-harm, or you're worried about the baby's safety, go to the nearest hospital emergency department or call 000** and ask for an ambulance. For help and support, call Lifeline on 13 11 14.

## Treatment and recovery

It's diagnosed by a psychiatrist and treated in hospital, sometimes in a mother and baby unit where the baby stays too; otherwise you or family will care for the baby until they're well enough to come home. Treatment is usually medicine, sometimes ECT for severe symptoms, and talking therapy. The first phase can take weeks or months. With the right treatment, a full recovery is likely.

Supporting someone through this is frightening. Counselling and support for you matter too: Beyond Blue (1300 22 4636), PANDA (1300 726 306), your GP or the hospital can help.
