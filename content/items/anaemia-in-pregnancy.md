Anaemia means your blood doesn't have enough healthy red blood cells or haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen around your body and to your baby. It's common, happening in about 1 in every 4 pregnancies, and your doctor can manage it.

## Why it happens

Low iron is the main cause. In pregnancy your blood volume increases so you and your baby get enough oxygen and nutrients, and your body needs extra iron. Low iron can come from not enough iron in your diet, poor absorption, or, less often, blood loss from parasites such as hookworm. Anaemia can also come from a lack of folate or vitamin B12, or a blood disorder such as thalassaemia or sickle cell disease.

It's more likely with a vegetarian or vegan diet, a poor diet, severe morning sickness, pregnancies close together, or a history of anaemia.

## How it's found

A full blood count, which checks your haemoglobin, is done at your first antenatal visit and usually again at about 28 weeks. If your haemoglobin is low, you may have more blood tests to find the cause. You might feel weak, tired or dizzy, or have headaches, shortness of breath or chest pain. Chest pain or severe trouble breathing: call 000.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. For iron deficiency anaemia, your doctor may recommend iron tablets. Some people get nausea or constipation from them. If the tablets don't help, your doctor may offer iron through an intravenous infusion; ask whether to expect any side effects. Your doctor will keep monitoring your haemoglobin. If there are concerns, your iron will be watched closely through the pregnancy.

Eating well may also help: iron is in meat, chicken and fish, legumes, nuts, wholegrain breads and cereals, and leafy green vegetables.

## Why it matters

Anaemia can make it harder for your body to cope with blood loss during labour and birth, and it can affect your baby's iron stores. Severe iron deficiency anaemia can cause heart failure. Anaemia from a lack of folate or vitamin B12 is linked to neural tube defects in the baby.
