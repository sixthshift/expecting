Your blood pressure is checked at every antenatal visit. In pregnancy it counts as high if the top number is 140 or more, or the bottom number is 90 or more ("140 over 90"). High blood pressure is a common problem in pregnancy and usually gets better after the birth. It may be a one-off, or a sign of pre-eclampsia, a more serious condition. The Royal Women's Hospital says high blood pressure should always be investigated.

## The kinds

- **Chronic hypertension**: high blood pressure you already had before pregnancy, or that's found in the first 20 weeks. It raises your risk of pre-eclampsia.
- **Gestational (pregnancy-induced) hypertension**: high blood pressure found after 20 weeks.
- **Pre-eclampsia**: a serious complication that causes high blood pressure and can affect your liver, kidneys, blood and brain. It can get worse very quickly, and you can have it with no symptoms at all.

You're at higher risk if you've had pre-eclampsia before or a close relative has, have kidney disease, diabetes or chronic hypertension, are over 40, are living with overweight or obesity, are carrying more than one baby, or conceived with IVF.

## Why it matters

High blood pressure can reduce blood flow to the placenta, so the baby may not get enough oxygen and nutrients. Untreated, it raises the baby's risk of low birth weight, premature birth, placental abruption and stillbirth. That's why it's important to notice and treat it early.

## What happens next

If your blood pressure is high, your doctor will make a plan for treatment and monitoring. You'll be offered extra check-ups:

- more frequent blood pressure readings
- blood and urine tests
- ultrasounds of the baby, the placenta and the fluid around the baby
- a CTG to check the baby's heart rate

If you took blood pressure medicine before pregnancy, ask your doctor which medicines are safe now. Your doctor can also prescribe medicines that lower your chance of pre-eclampsia. Quitting smoking and vaping, eating well, exercising and keeping a healthy weight help too.

If there are signs of pre-eclampsia, a team may care for you, including an obstetrician, midwife, obstetric physician, anaesthetist and a newborn specialist. It's usually best to plan to give birth at a large maternity hospital. If things worsen, you may be monitored in hospital and the baby may need to be born early. After the birth you should have extra blood pressure checks for several weeks.

## When to get help

**Seek medical attention urgently, or go to your local emergency department**, if you have a severe headache that doesn't get better with painkillers, blurred vision or flashing lights, sudden swelling of your hands, feet or face, or pain under your ribs or in your upper tummy.
