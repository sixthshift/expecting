An induction is when your midwife or doctor starts labour for you, with your consent. The Royal Women's Hospital says about one in three have one. You may be offered it if staying pregnant is riskier for you or your baby than the birth coming sooner. It's an offer: the decision is always yours, you can change your mind at any time, and you can ask for a second opinion.

## The options

**Why it's offered.** Common reasons include going past your due date (Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says 41 to 42 weeks), your waters breaking without contractions starting within 24 to 48 hours, diabetes or gestational diabetes, blood pressure problems such as pre-eclampsia, group B strep with your waters broken, twins (depending on your situation), a placenta that may not be working as it should, or worries about the baby's growth, movements or fluid.

**How it's done.** First your midwife or doctor examines your cervix. That decides the method, and you may need one or a combination:

- **Prostaglandin**, a gel or pessary in the vagina to soften and open the cervix.
- **A balloon catheter**, a small water-filled balloon that presses on the cervix to open it.
- **Breaking the waters** (artificial rupture of membranes), once the cervix is ready.
- **An oxytocin drip** in your arm, to start contractions, once the waters have broken.

**Waiting.** Some people choose to wait and see whether labour starts on its own. Your doctor may ask you to weigh that the risk of stillbirth and other problems rises the longer a pregnancy goes past term.

## Things to weigh

- **Time.** From starting to the birth can take a few hours or more than 24 hours, usually in hospital.
- **How it feels.** Induced contractions can build up quickly and be more intense than in a labour that starts by itself. You're more likely to have continuous monitoring and more vaginal examinations, and to want pain relief sooner. There's usually no restriction on what pain relief you can have.
- **Moving around.** With a drip and a CTG monitor it's hard to move about, and a bath isn't possible.
- **Assisted birth and caesarean.** Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says there's no difference in how often forceps or vacuum are used. RANZCOG says that, done appropriately, induction doesn't increase the chance of interventions. The Royal Women's says an induction for reasons other than a prolonged pregnancy may increase the chance of an emergency caesarean.
- **It may not work.** If it doesn't, your midwife or doctor may suggest another method or recommend a caesarean.
- **How you feel afterwards.** Some people feel less satisfied with an induced birth, especially if they wanted a more natural one. Those feelings are valid.

## When to decide

When it's offered. Ask:

- Why do I need an induction, and why now?
- What happens if I wait for labour to start on its own?
- What are my other options?
- How will you induce my labour, and what are the risks?
- What are my options for pain relief?

If you're overdue, ask your team how quickly you need to decide. Whatever you choose, tell them straight away if you notice the baby moving less.
