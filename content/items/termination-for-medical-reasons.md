Termination for medical reasons means ending a wanted pregnancy because of serious concerns about the baby's development or the pregnant parent's health. It often comes after unexpected news, when you were getting ready to welcome your baby, and you may have had to take in complex medical information and make a deeply personal decision in a short time. Even a decision made with care, love and medical advice can leave a lasting mark. You may grieve your baby and the future you'd imagined.

## How you might feel

There's no right or wrong way. You might feel shock and numbness, deep sadness and emptiness, guilt and self-blame, anxiety, anger, relief alongside grief, trouble making sense of what happened, worry about future pregnancies, and isolation, especially if few people knew. Hormones, bleeding, pain and exhaustion can make coping harder, and due dates, anniversaries, appointments and other people's pregnancy news can bring it back.

## What happens

How a pregnancy is ended depends on how many weeks along you are: by medicine, or by a surgical procedure. The law differs between states and territories, and your doctor may need approval if you are more than 20 weeks. Counselling is available before and after, and it's optional.

## Honouring your baby

Some people find rituals or keepsakes comforting; others aren't sure what they need, and that's okay. You might:

- keep an ultrasound image, the hospital bracelet, a letter or a memory box
- name your baby, or write them a letter
- mark important dates, such as lighting a candle
- take part in cultural, spiritual or religious rituals that matter to you

## The two of you

Partners can be grieving while trying to support you, and you may grieve, talk and need support differently. That doesn't mean either of you is doing it wrong. Let trusted people know what helps: practical help, quiet company, or simply having your baby acknowledged.

## Looking after yourself

Rest, eat and drink regularly, accept help with meals and the house, talk to someone you trust, limit time with people who are dismissive, and take the pressure off "moving on". Ask for clear medical information and follow-up about your recovery or future pregnancies.

## Getting more support

Reach out if your distress feels overwhelming, you're struggling with daily life, or reminders leave you panicked or shut down. Your partner may need support too.

- your GP, obstetrician or midwife, who can refer you on
- Gidget Foundation, with psychological therapy for pregnancy loss, including termination
- Miscarriage Australia, PANDA, Pink Elephants, Red Nose Grief and Loss Support, and the Royal Women's Hospital

## When to get help

If you think you have an infection after a termination (fever, chills, feeling unwell, unpleasant-smelling vaginal discharge), get urgent medical attention. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 000. For urgent mental health support, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636.
